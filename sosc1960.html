Lecture 10: Would You Trust Your Eyes or Follow the Crowd?

This lecture examines social influence (社會影響) — how other people shape what we do, think, and say. It moves from unconscious mimicry, through the pressure to conform and comply, to full obedience to authority, and closes with practical applications that harness these mechanisms for behaviour change. The through-line is that humans are profoundly social: even trivial cues from strangers can override our own perceptions.

1. Social Influence and Automatic Mimicry

  • Social influence (社會影響) — the observation that an individual's behaviour is shaped by the actions of other people or groups; can occur explicitly (consciously) or implicitly (unconsciously).

Automatic mimicry (自動模仿):

  • Adoption of others' postures (姿勢), gestures (手勢), and mannerisms (舉止) without awareness or intent (Chartrand & Bargh, 1999).
  • Classic study: participants worked alongside a confederate (同謀者) who either rubbed their face or shook their foot; participants were more likely to copy the same gesture than to display a different one.
  • Data: face-rub frequency ~0.73 (mimicked confederate rubbing face) vs. ~0.45 (confederate shaking foot); foot-shake ~0.57 vs. ~0.35 — mirror effect is consistent across gestures.
  • Automatic mimicry facilitates communication (溝通) and fosters relationships (Lakin et al., 2003).
  • Post-interaction ratings: mimicked participants reported higher liking (喜好) and smoother interaction (互動流暢度) than non-mimicked participants.
  • Pro-social spillover: participants who had been mimicked were significantly more likely to help pick up accidentally dropped pens within 10 seconds (Van Baaren et al., 2004).

Exam trap: Automatic mimicry is unconscious — it is NOT deliberate imitation. Students often conflate it with conscious copying. The key finding is that it happens without awareness or intent.

2. Conformity

  • Conformity (從眾) — the act of changing one's behaviour to match the responses of others (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004).

Asch line-judgment studies:

  • Task was deliberately easy: 99% of individuals gave the correct answer when tested alone.
  • In 12 of 18 trials, all other group members (confederates) unanimously gave the wrong answer.
  • Overall error rate: 36.8% — participants conformed and gave the wrong answer more than a third of the time.
  • Group size matters: conformity (從眾率) increased as the number of opponents increased, but plateaued after ~3–4 opponents.
  • Presence of a dissenter (異見者): even one person giving the correct answer sharply reduced conformity.

Modern replications:

  • Swiss replication (Franzen & Mader, 2023): observed error rate 33%; incentivizing (獎勵) correct answers reduced rate to 25% — conformity persists but is responsive to incentives.
  • Political opinion conformity: participants were more likely to answer "Yes" to policy questions when group members also said "Yes"; conformity rate ~38%.

Two mechanisms driving conformity:

  • Informational influence (資訊性影響) — conformity from wanting to form an accurate interpretation and behave correctly; dominant when the situation is ambiguous.
  • Normative influence (規範性影響) — conformity from concern about what others think of us; driven by social approval and belonging.
  • Cultural differences (文化差異): collectivistic (集體主義) societies show more conformity than individualistic ones (Bond & Smith, 1996).

Exam trap: Informational and normative influence are distinct. Informational influence is about being correct; normative influence is about being liked/accepted. Do not merge them into one explanation.

3. Compliance

  • Compliance (順從) — acquiescence or positive response to a direct or covert request (隱性請求), such as a sales pitch (推銷) or donation solicitation (募款).

Cialdini's (2006) six universal principles of compliance (六大普世順從原則):

Exam trap: Social Proof is classified under informational influence, not normative influence. Students often assign it to normative influence because it involves "others" — but the mechanism is about accuracy beliefs, not approval-seeking.

4. Obedience

  • Obedience (服從) — adherence to commands or instructions from someone with higher status or authority (Milgram, 1963).
  • People may invoke "just following orders" as a justification for unethical or unlawful acts — illustrated by Adolf Eichmann's defence at his 1962 trial for his role in the Holocaust.

Milgram's shock experiment:

  • Participants were instructed to deliver increasingly strong electric shocks (電擊) to a "learner" (confederate) ostensibly as part of a learning study.
  • Pre-study predictions by laypeople: almost no participant would deliver the maximum shock — prediction proved dramatically wrong.
  • Original obedience rate (Milgram, 1974, average across Experiments 1,2,3,5,6,8,10): 56.43%.
  • U.S. studies range from 30% (Podd, 1970) to 91% (Ring et al., 1970); U.S. mean ~60.94%.
  • Foreign studies mean ~65.94%; range from 28% (Australia, Kilham & Mann, 1974) to 87.5% (South Africa, Edwards et al., 1969).
  • Cross-cultural review (Blass, 2012): average obedience scores are in roughly the same range as Milgram's original findings regardless of culture.

Replication (Burger, 2009):

  • Majority of participants continued after 150 volts (when confederate yelled in pain).
  • Seeing a defying model (拒從楷模) did not substantially reduce obedience: base refusal condition 70.0% continued; modelled refusal condition 63.3% continued; Milgram Experiment 5 82.5% continued.

Defiance (抗命) and whistle-blowing (揭發):

  • A minority choose to defy unjust authority (不公正權威), citing ethics (道德), compassion (同情心), or situational incongruity as reasons (Bocchiaro & Zimbardo, 2010).
  • Participant reasons for stopping in Milgram's study: (1) worry about the performer's health; (2) moral-ethical decision; (3) empathy; (4) situational incongruity.
  • An even smaller subset goes further by whistle-blowing — reporting ethically questionable orders to higher authorities (Bocchiaro et al., 2012).

Exam trap: Obedience rates did NOT differ dramatically across cultures in cross-cultural reviews — they remain in broadly the same range. Do not claim the Milgram effect is culture-specific.

5. Applications: Leveraging Social Influence

Two types of normative messages (規範訊息) used in interventions:

  • Descriptive norms (描述性規範) — information about what most people actually do (e.g., "most people conserve water").
  • Injunctive/prescriptive norms (命令性/規定性規範) — information about what most people approve or disapprove of (e.g., "most people believe conserving water is important").

Elevator vs. stairs study (Burger & Shelton, 2011):

  • Researchers counted elevator use to go up one or two floors over three weeks.
  • Posting a norm-sign ("more than 90% of the time, people in this building use the stairs") in Week 2 reduced elevator use by 46% (from ~15% to ~8%).
  • Exercise-sign condition: minimal change across weeks (~15.1%, 13.3%, 13.4%).
  • No-sign (對照組) condition: stable at ~35–38%.

Hotel towel reuse study (Goldstein et al., 2008):

  • Descriptive norm message ("Almost 75% of guests reuse their towels") yielded a towel reuse rate of 44.1%.
  • Standard environmental protection message yielded 35.1% reuse.
  • Descriptive norm outperformed standard messaging by ~9 percentage points.

Lecture 9: Why Do We Need Friends?

This lecture asks why humans form and maintain social bonds, drawing on four theoretical traditions: need-to-belong theory, self-determination theory, Maslow's hierarchy, and evolutionary and terror management accounts. It then examines the substantial health consequences of social connection and the psychological and behavioral fallout when the need to belong is thwarted through exclusion or ostracism.

1. Theories of Belonging: Need-to-Belong and Self-Determination Theory

Need-to-Belong Theory (歸屬需求理論) — Baumeister & Leary (1995):

  • Humans are fundamentally and pervasively motivated by a need to belong (歸屬感).
  • People seek frequent, affectively positive interactions (正向互動) within long-term, caring relationships.
  • Lack of belongingness (缺乏歸屬感) constitutes severe deprivation (嚴重剝奪) and causes a variety of ill effects.
  • People actively try to preserve relationships and avoid ending them, even destructive ones.
  • Humans experience distress (心理痛苦) when a relationship ends; when a functional group disbands, members often still want to hold on to the bond formed (Lacoursiere, 1980).

Self-Determination Theory (自我決定理論) — Ryan & Deci (2000):

  • Three inherent psychological needs (內在心理需求):
    • Competence (勝任感) — mastering the world and feeling effective.
    • Relatedness (連結感) — being connected to and experiencing care for others.
    • Autonomy (自主性) — experiencing volition and acting in accord with one's sense of self.
  • Optimal well-being (最佳幸福感) results when all three needs are satisfied simultaneously.
  • Patrick et al. (2007): couples reported greater relationship satisfaction when both partners experienced greater relatedness-need fulfillment (關係需求滿足).
  • Gagne & Deci (2005): satisfaction of employees' relatedness needs predicted work engagement (工作投入), performance, and well-being on the job.
Exam trap: SDT has three needs, not two. Students often forget autonomy and list only competence and relatedness. All three must be satisfied for optimal well-being.

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs (需求層次理論) — Maslow (1970):

  • Humans are motivated by a pyramid (金字塔) of needs, from basic survival upward to self-actualization (自我實現).
  • Lower-level needs must be satisfied before higher-level growth needs (成長需求) can be focused on.
  • Levels (bottom to top): Physiological (生理需求) → Safety and security (安全需求) → Belongingness and love (歸屬與愛) → Esteem (尊重需求) → Cognitive (認知需求) → Aesthetic (審美需求) → Self-actualization.
  • Failure to meet lower needs causes regression (退行) to more basic levels; meeting them enables progression (進步) upward.
  • Tay & Diener (2011), study of 123 nations: basic and safety needs were most likely fulfilled first among those with limited overall need fulfillment; social needs came next — consistent with Maslow's ordering.

2. Evolutionary and Terror Management Perspectives

Evolutionary Perspective (演化觀點):

  • Motivation (動機) is a product of evolution (演化); social drives are not culturally arbitrary.
  • Social bonds (社會連結) and cooperative behavior (合作行為) solved recurrent adaptive challenges (適應性挑戰):
    • Predation (掠食) avoidance.
    • Resource acquisition (資源獲取).
    • Offspring care (後代照顧).
    • Coalitionary conflict (聯盟衝突).
  • These pressures selected for intrinsic motivation (內在動機) to bond and cooperate.

Terror Management Theory (恐懼管理理論):

  • The juxtaposition (並置) of the desire for survival and awareness of death gives rise to death terror (死亡恐懼).
  • Close relationships play an important role in buffering death anxiety (緩衝死亡焦慮) (Plusnin et al., 2018).
  • Reminders of death (死亡提示) increase attempts to initiate new close relationships and maintain existing ones.
  • Post-9/11 evidence (Morgan et al., 2011; Saad, 2001; Skitka et al., 2004; Roberts, 2002):
    • 60% of Americans reported their relationships were stronger after the attack.
    • 40% increased attempts to do nice things for others.
    • A majority displayed the American flag, driven by patriotism — a form of symbolic group affiliation (群體認同).
    • Strangers spontaneously connected in the streets, showing heightened community bonding (社區連結).
Exam trap: Terror management theory is about death anxiety (死亡焦慮) driving social connection — not about fear of social rejection. Do not confuse it with ostracism research.

3. Social Connection and Health

Core finding (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2017): having high-quality close relationships (高品質親密關係) and feeling connected to others is associated with decreased risk for:

  • All-cause mortality (全因死亡率).
  • Disease morbidities (疾病發病率) across multiple conditions.

Social connection (社會連結) has three dimensions (Holt-Lundstad et al., 2021):

DimensionDefinitionExamples
Structure (結構)Existence of relationships and their rolesNetwork size, marital status, living alone
Function (功能)Actual or perceived support or inclusionPerceived social support, received support
Quality (品質)Positive and negative qualities of relationshipsRelationship satisfaction, strain, ambivalence

Mortality risk comparison (Holt-Lundstad et al., 2017): the protective effect (保護效果) of social connection on mortality is comparable to — or exceeds — that of known health risk factor interventions:

  • Exceeds: abstaining from excessive alcohol, flu vaccination, controlling air pollution, and managing obesity.
  • Comparable to: smoking cessation among cardiac patients, physical activity.
  • Complex integration measures of social integration show the strongest protective effect.

Pathways from social connection to morbidity and mortality (Holt-Lundstad, 2021):

  • Psychological factors (心理因素): perceived stress (感知壓力), depression, resilience (韌性), meaning/purpose, hopefulness, sense of safety.
  • Behavioral factors (行為因素): sleep quality, physical activity, nutrition, smoking, medical adherence (服藥依從性).
  • Biological factors (生物因素): biomarkers, cardiovascular (心血管), neuroendocrine (神經內分泌), and immune functioning (免疫功能).
  • Risk factors that moderate pathways: functional impairments, stigma, life transitions (生命轉變), role loss, societal barriers (社會障礙).
  • Outcomes: coronary heart disease (冠心病), stroke (中風), diabetes (糖尿病), all-cause mortality.
Exam trap: Social connection operates via psychological, behavioral, AND biological pathways — not just one. Exam questions may ask you to name all three pathway types.

4. When the Need Is Thwarted: Social Exclusion and Ostracism

General consequences of social exclusion (社會排斥) / ostracism (排斥他人) (Williams & Nida, 2011):

  • Negative physiological (生理), affective (情感), cognitive (認知), and behavioral (行為) consequences are unleashed.
  • Excluded people attempt to reaffiliate (重新連結) with others.

Cyberball study (網路接球研究) — Williams et al. (2000):

  • Participants played an online disc-throwing game with two bogus players (虛假玩家).
  • Ostracized participants showed:
    • Poorer mood (情緒下降).
    • Greater conformity (從眾性) to others' wrong judgments.
    • Heightened activity in brain cortex area associated with pain (疼痛相關腦區活化).

Rejection and aggression study — Twenge et al. (2001):

  • After an acquaintance session, participants were told they were either accepted ("everyone chose you") or rejected ("no one chose you").
  • Rejected participants were later more aggressive (攻擊性更強) in punishing game losers — higher noise intensity and longer noise duration.

Rejection and school violence — Leary et al. (2003):

  • In at least 12 of 15 school shooting incidents (1995–2001), perpetrators had experienced a pattern of malicious teasing or bullying (惡意欺凌).
  • Victims included individuals who had teased, bullied, or rejected the shooter.
  • In about half the episodes, the perpetrator had also experienced a recent romantic breakup (分手) or other rejecting event.

Exclusion and smile sensitivity — Bernstein et al. (2008):

  • Participants recalled either a "rejected/excluded" or "accepted/included" experience, then viewed 20 faces (10 fake smiles, 10 real smiles).
  • Rejected participants showed enhanced sensitivity (敏感度提升) to distinguish genuine happy expressions from fake ones.
  • Interpretation: exclusion heightens social signal detection (社交信號偵測) to facilitate reaffiliation.

Anthropomorphism as compensation (擬人化補償) — Epley et al. (2007, 2008):

  • When lacking social connection (缺乏社會連結), people compensate by anthropomorphizing nonhumans (將非人類擬人化) — perceiving them as humanlike.
  • Lonely people perceive more mental states (intentions, emotions) in gadgets than non-lonely people.
  • Participants shown a loneliness-inducing film segment (Cast Away) reported stronger belief in supernatural agents (超自然力量) and used more human traits to describe pets.
  • Excluded participants (vs. included) reported more positive attitude toward M&M's candy when the brand was anthropomorphized (Chen et al., 2008).
Exam trap: Anthropomorphism (擬人化) here is a compensatory mechanism (補償機制) for lost belonging — not simply a general cognitive bias. The cause is social exclusion, not curiosity or creativity.
Exam trap: The brain pain-area activation in the Cyberball study shows that social exclusion activates the same neural circuits (神經迴路) as physical pain — do not describe this as mere metaphor.
Exam Traps Summary
  • SDT has three needs (competence, relatedness, autonomy) — not two. All three must be satisfied for optimal well-being.
  • In Maslow's hierarchy, belongingness is level 3, not level 2. Safety comes before social needs.
  • Terror management theory links death anxiety to social bonding — do not confuse it with fear of social rejection or ostracism research.
  • Social connection operates via psychological, behavioral, AND biological pathways to affect morbidity and mortality — not just one pathway.
  • Social connection has three dimensions (structure, function, quality) — exam questions may ask you to define all three.
  • The Cyberball pain-area activation is real neural overlap with physical pain — not a metaphor.
  • Anthropomorphism in excluded individuals is a compensatory mechanism for lost belonging — not a general cognitive tendency unrelated to social needs.
Top things to memorize

  2. SDT (Ryan & Deci, 2000): three needs — competence, relatedness, autonomy; optimal well-being requires all three.
  3. Maslow's hierarchy (1970): 7 levels from physiological to self-actualization; belongingness is level 3; lower needs must be met first.
  4. Terror management theory: death awareness drives affiliation; post-9/11 data: 60% reported stronger relationships, 40% increased prosocial acts.
  5. Three dimensions of social connection: structure (network, roles), function (support), quality (satisfaction, strain).
  6. Holt-Lunstad et al. (2017): social connection's protective effect on mortality equals or exceeds smoking, alcohol, obesity, and air pollution interventions.
  7. Pathways to health: psychological (stress, depression), behavioral (sleep, activity), biological (cardiovascular, immune, neuroendocrine).
  8. Cyberball (Williams et al., 2000): ostracism causes poor mood, conformity, and pain-area brain activation.
  9. Rejection increases aggression: rejected participants gave higher noise intensity and duration to punish game losers (Twenge et al., 2001).
  10. Leary et al. (2003): 12 of 15 school shooters had experienced chronic bullying/rejection; ~half had a recent romantic breakup.
  11. Exclusion heightens smile authenticity detection (Bernstein et al., 2008) — a reaffiliation-seeking mechanism.
  12. Anthropomorphism compensates for social exclusion — lonely/excluded people project human traits onto gadgets, pets, and brands (Epley et al., 2007, 2008; Chen et al., 2008).

Lecture 8: Why Do Some People Handle Stress Better Than Others?

Stress (壓力) is an unavoidable part of life, but people differ enormously in how well they manage it. This lecture maps the full arc: what stress is and where it comes from, how the body and mind react to it, and what strategies and personal traits allow some individuals to cope effectively while others are overwhelmed. The key throughline is that both coping flexibility and psychological resilience explain why identical stressors produce very different outcomes across people.

1. What Is Stress and Where Does It Come From?

Stress (壓力) is defined as the state that arises "when individuals perceive that they cannot adequately cope with the demands being made on them or with threats to their well-being" (Lazarus, 1966). Key elements:

  • Stress is perceptual (知覺的) — the same event can be stressful or not depending on how an individual appraises it.
  • Stressors (壓力源) — the sources of stress; any demand on a person or threat to their well-being.

Four main categories of stressors:

  • Catastrophes (災難性事件) — unpredictable, large-scale events threatening many people at once (e.g., natural disasters, terrorist attacks); linked to high rates of PTSD.
  • Traumatic events (創傷性事件) — situations involving actual or threatened death or serious injury (e.g., military combat, physical assault, abuse, accidents).
  • Major life events (重大生活事件) — typically have immediate consequences that fade with time (e.g., death of a family member, job loss); measured by the Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS; Holmes & Rahe, 1967) in "life change units" (生活改變單位); higher cumulative scores predict depression and physical illness.
  • Hassles (日常困擾) — everyday minor annoyances that strain coping ability (e.g., nasty boss, relationship problems); vary by age group.

Occupational stress (職業壓力) — stress from unpleasant, demanding, or unsafe working conditions; examples by occupation:

  • Social workers — little positive feedback, unsafe environments, bureaucracy, excessive paperwork, work overload.
  • Teachers — excessive paperwork, lack of supplies, physical violence threat, lack of parental/administrative support.
  • Nurses — work overload, heavy physical demands, dealing with death, interpersonal conflict with physicians.
  • Emergency medical workers — unpredictable/extreme job nature, inexperience.
  • Clerical/secretarial workers — few advancement opportunities, unsupportive supervisors, lack of perceived control.

Professional athletes (職業運動員) face four categories of organizational stressor (Arnold & Fletcher, 2012): (1) leadership and personnel issues, (2) logistical and environmental issues, (3) performance and personal issues, (4) cultural and team issues.

PTSD (創傷後壓力症候群; post-traumatic stress disorder) after catastrophes:

  • Characterized by flashbacks, avoidance of reminders, recurrent dreams, and chronic physiological arousal (慢性生理激發).
  • After 9/11: 20% of residents near Ground Zero developed PTSD vs. 7.5% of residents further north (Galea et al., 2002).
  • Pooled prevalence (pooled prevalence 彙總盛行率) of PTSD in the general population within the first year after a natural hazard: ~26% (Kip et al., 2024; 14,344 participants across 27 studies).
Exam trap: Major life events are not always negative — "outstanding personal achievement" and "beginning or ending school" also carry life change units and contribute to cumulative stress scores.

2. How We Appraise Stress

Stress is subjective (主觀的); not all people react the same way to the same event (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Two-stage appraisal (評估) process:

  • Primary appraisal (初級評估) — perception of whether an event is threatening or not. Two outcomes:
    • Challenge (挑戰) — perceived potential for gain or growth.
    • Threat (威脅) — perceived potential for harm, loss, or negative consequences.
  • Secondary appraisal (次級評估) — perception of one's own ability (能力) to cope with the event; evaluation of available options and their effectiveness.
    • Effective options available → low threat perception.
    • Ineffective or no options → high threat perception.
Exam trap: Primary appraisal is about whether an event is threatening; secondary appraisal is about whether you can cope with it. These are distinct cognitive steps (認知步驟), not synonyms.

3. Health Effects of Stress

Stress has both beneficial and harmful effects on health (健康):

  • Adaptive short-term stress — mobilizes the body via the fight-or-flight response (戰鬥或逃跑反應): hormone secretions by adrenal glands (腎上腺), increased heart rate (心率) and blood pressure (血壓), changes in skin conductance (皮膚導電性).
  • Prolonged exposure (長期暴露) — decline of bodily functioning, deterioration of body tissues, decreased immune functioning (免疫功能).

General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) (一般適應症候群; Selye, 1956) — three stages:

  • Stage 1 — Alarm and Mobilization (警覺與動員): body detects stressor and mounts initial response.
  • Stage 2 — Resistance (抵抗): body meets and resists the stressor; coping is active.
  • Stage 3 — Exhaustion (衰竭): when coping is inadequate, negative consequences including illness emerge.

Stress and immune system (免疫系統):

  • Cohen et al. (1991): participants given nasal drops containing respiratory virus — high-stress individuals were more likely to develop colds, even after controlling for personality and health.
  • During exams (考試期間), students with stronger social support had higher antibody (抗體) levels (Jemmott & Magloire, 1988).

Psychoneuroimmunology (心理神經免疫學) — the study of relationships among psychological factors, the immune system, and the brain. Stress operates through three pathways:

Three main coping strategies (應對策略) differ in approach and suitability:

Avoidance-oriented coping (迴避型應對) Avoiding actions to solve problems; wishful thinking, direct escape, substance use, overeating Generally maladaptive (適應不良的); postponing dealing with the situation worsens the problem and creates more stress
Problem-focused coping (問題導向應對) Taking action to reduce the impact of the stressor or prevent recurrence; consulting professionals, acquiring information Modifiable or controllable circumstances (可控情況)
Emotion-focused coping (情緒導向應對) Managing and regulating emotions in the face of stress; seeking positive outlook, accepting sympathy, diary writing Unchangeable or uncertain circumstances (無法改變的情況)

Coping flexibility (應對靈活性; Cheng, 2001) — the ability to flexibly deploy different coping strategies across distinct stressful contexts:

  • Problem-focused coping in an uncontrollable situation can elicit anxiety (焦慮).
  • Emotion-focused coping in a controllable situation may distract from solving the actual problem.
  • The key is matching strategy to situational demands (情境需求), not using one approach universally.
Exam trap: Emotion-focused coping is not inherently inferior to problem-focused coping. In genuinely uncontrollable situations, emotion regulation (情緒調節) is the more adaptive choice.

5. Stress Reduction and Social Support

Two main stress reduction/management techniques (壓力減緩技術):

  • Relaxation exercises (放鬆練習) — calm arousal and reduce intensity of negative emotions through meditation (冥想), breathing techniques, guided imagery (引導意象), and contact with nature.
  • Biofeedback (生物回饋) — a technique where individuals are shown their own bodily signals (e.g., heart rate) and taught strategies to alter those signals voluntarily.

Nature and stress reduction:

  • Natural environments (自然環境) have been advantageous to survival throughout evolutionary history.
  • Stress reduction theory (壓力減緩理論; Ulrich, 1991) — nature automatically elicits positive reactions tied to suppression of sympathetic nervous system (交感神經系統) activity.
  • Nature-inspired VR simulations can effectively help patients cope with discomfort and stress during painful medical procedures (Scates et al., 2020).

Social support (社會支持) — a network of caring, interested others who facilitate coping and serve as a buffer against the negative impacts of stress. Three types:

  • Informational support (資訊性支持) — advice and information.
  • Materialistic support (物質性支持) — goods and services.
  • Emotional support (情感性支持) — feeling that one is important and valued.
  • Students with stronger social support during exams had higher antibody levels (Jemmott & Magloire, 1988).
  • Women exposed to stress showed less physiological reaction when in the company of their pets (Allen et al., 1991) — pets can serve as a source of social support.

6. Psychological Resilience

Psychological resilience (心理韌性) — a relatively stable trait characterized by the ability to overcome, steer through, and bounce back from adversity (逆境; Block & Kremen, 1996).

Key factors contributing to resilience, based on a study of Olympic champions (Fletcher & Sarkar, 2012):

  • Psychological resilience emerges from a host of psychological factors.
  • These factors influence challenge appraisal (挑戰評估) and meta-cognitions (元認知) — thinking about one's own thoughts and coping strategies.
  • Positive personality (積極人格) — stable, optimistic character traits.
  • Confidence (自信心) — belief in one's ability to succeed under pressure.
  • Perceived social support (感知到的社會支持) — the belief that support is available, even if not actively used.
  • The combination of these factors promotes facilitative optimal performance responses (促進最佳表現反應).

Resilience model pathway: Psychological Factors → Challenge Appraisal + Meta-Cognitions → Confidence + Perceived Social Support → Facilitative Responses → Optimal Performance.

Exam trap: Psychological resilience is described as a relatively stable trait, not a skill learned in the moment. However, it is built from modifiable psychological factors (心理因素) — it is not purely innate.
Exam Traps Summary
  • Major life events include positive events (e.g., personal achievement, school changes) that still carry life change units — stress is not only from negative events.
  • Primary appraisal = "Is this threatening?" Secondary appraisal = "Can I cope?" Do not confuse them or treat them as synonymous.
  • GAS exhaustion stage = depletion of coping resources leading to illness, not ordinary fatigue.
  • Emotion-focused coping is not inferior — it is adaptive in genuinely uncontrollable situations; problem-focused coping in those situations can actually increase anxiety.
  • Psychological resilience is a relatively stable trait but emerges from modifiable psychological factors — not purely innate or fixed.
Top things to memorize
  1. Lazarus (1966) definition of stress — perception of inability to cope with demands or threats.
  2. Four stressor types: catastrophes, traumatic events, major life events, hassles.
  3. General Adaptation Syndrome (Selye, 1956) — three stages: alarm, resistance, exhaustion.
  4. Primary appraisal (challenge vs. threat) vs. secondary appraisal (coping options available).
  5. Three coping strategies: avoidance-oriented, problem-focused, emotion-focused — and when each is appropriate.
  6. Coping flexibility (Cheng, 2001) — matching strategy to situational controllability.
  7. Psychoneuroimmunology — study of psychological factors, immune system, and brain.
  8. Three pathways of stress on health: direct physiological, harmful behaviors, indirect health-related behaviors.
  9. Three types of social support: informational, materialistic, emotional.
  10. Stress reduction theory (Ulrich, 1991) — nature suppresses sympathetic nervous system activity.
  11. Biofeedback — technique of showing bodily signals and teaching voluntary alteration.
  12. Psychological resilience (Block & Kremen, 1996) — stable trait; built from positive personality, confidence, challenge appraisal, meta-cognitions, perceived social support (Fletcher & Sarkar, 2012).

Lecture 7: What Makes a Good Parent?

This lecture examines the science of parenting by exploring how parenting styles (權威式、專制式、放任式、漠不關心式), attachment security (依附安全感), and intergenerational transmission (代際傳遞) shape child development across the lifespan. Two key dimensions — responsiveness and demandingness — underpin most parenting research, while cultural context challenges the assumption that any single style is universally best. Understanding these mechanisms reveals not only how parents influence children, but how children can also influence parents.

1. Parenting Styles

Parenting styles (養育風格) are standard strategies parents use in child rearing (Baumrind, 1991). They are defined by two dimensions (維度): responsiveness (回應性) — how warmly parents react to children's needs — and demandingness (要求性) — how much control or discipline parents impose.

Style Responsiveness Demandingness Parent Behavior Child Outcomes
Authoritative (權威式) High High Firm, sets limits and goals, uses reasoning, encourages independence Good social skills, likable, self-reliant, independent
Authoritarian (專制式) Low High Rigid, punitive, strict standards Unsociable, unfriendly, withdrawn
Permissive (放任式) High Low Lax, inconsistent, undemanding Immature, moody, dependent, low self-control
Uninvolved (漠不關心式) Low Low Detached emotionally, provides only basic necessities Indifferent, rejecting behavior
  • Authoritative parenting is traditionally assumed to be most beneficial for child development.
  • Example of authoritative style: "You'll need to clean your room before we can go out to the restaurant. As soon as you finish, we'll leave."
  • Example of authoritarian style: "If you don't clean your room, I'm going to take away your iPod for good and ground you."
  • Example of permissive style: "It might be good to clean your room, but I guess it can wait."
  • Example of uninvolved style: "I couldn't care less if your room is a pigsty."
Exam trap: Authoritative (權威式) and Authoritarian (專制式) sound similar but are opposites. Authoritative = high responsiveness + high demandingness → positive outcomes. Authoritarian = low responsiveness + high demandingness → negative outcomes.

2. Parenting Dimensions: Psychological vs. Behavioral Control

Beyond broad styles, parenting can be analyzed along two specific control dimensions (控制維度) that predict development outcomes differently.

  • Psychological control (心理控制) — parents' attempts to limit or manipulate children's thoughts and feelings; often intrusive (侵入性的).
  • Examples of psychological control: guilt induction (罪惡感誘導), love withdrawal (撤回愛), shaming (羞辱), invalidation of feelings (否定感受).
  • Psychological control generally predicts worse developmental outcomes.
  • Behavioral control (行為控制) — parents' attempts to set clear rules and monitor children's activities.
  • Examples of behavioral control: setting rules, solicitation of information or disclosure (要求披露), media monitoring (媒體監控).
  • Behavioral control generally predicts better developmental outcomes.
Exam trap: Both psychological and behavioral control are forms of "control," but they predict opposite outcomes. Do not treat all parental control as equally harmful.

3. Cultural Differences in Parenting

A key paradox (悖論) in cross-cultural parenting research: Chinese parenting is often described as "controlling" or "authoritarian," yet Chinese students perform well academically — contradicting Western findings that link authoritarian style to poor school achievement among European-Americans (Chao, 1994).

  • "Battle Hymn of the Tiger Mother" (Chua, 2011) brought global attention to strict Chinese parenting practices.
  • Authoritarian parenting is widespread in non-Western cultures and among lower socioeconomic status (社會經濟地位) and minority parents in the US.
  • Authoritarian and controlling parenting is less maladaptive (適應不良) or not maladaptive in some contexts (Smetana, 2017).
  • Contexts where authoritarian parenting may be adaptive: Asian societies; dangerous or violent neighborhoods.
  • Implication: parenting style outcomes are not universal — cultural and environmental context (文化與環境背景) must be considered.

4. Attachment

Attachment (依附) is a strong emotional connection with those to whom we feel closest, such as parents. It ensures that infants do not stray too far from caretakers and thereby benefit from warmth, security, and guidance (Bowlby, 1973). Security in attachment varies between infants.

The Ainsworth Strange Situation (陌生情境實驗; Ainsworth et al., 1978) is the key paradigm for measuring attachment. Researchers observe the infant's exploratory behavior when the mother is present, reactions to a stranger, distress upon separation, and reunion behavior.

  • Procedure steps: (1) Baby plays with toys while mother is present. (2) Stranger enters. (3) Mother leaves. (4) Stranger tries to interact with baby. (5) Mother returns ("first reunion") and stranger leaves. (6) Mother leaves again. (7) Stranger returns. (8) Mother returns ("second reunion").
Attachment Type Prevalence Behavior on Separation Behavior on Reunion
Secure (安全型) ~60% Becomes upset when parent leaves Actively seeks parent; easily comforted
Insecure-Avoidant (不安全-迴避型) ~20% Shows no distress when mother leaves Actively avoids contact; may turn attention elsewhere
Insecure-Anxious/Resistant (不安全-焦慮抗拒型) ~20% Becomes extremely distressed Difficult to soothe; conflicting behaviors — seeks comfort but also "punishes" parent

Long-term outcomes of early attachment (依附的長期影響): early attachment experiences shape how children interpret and respond to social experiences in later life, affecting social and emotional functioning.

  • Secure attachment predicts: more sociable and liked; stronger friendships; more advanced emotional understanding (情感理解); greater persistence in challenging tasks; less psychopathology (心理病理), aggression, and disruption (Bowlby, 2005).
  • Some consistency between attachment in childhood and attachment in adulthood.
  • Early attachment does not determine fate but provides the foundation (基礎) for subsequent experiences.
  • In adulthood, people rely more on close friends and romantic partners (戀愛伴侶) for attachment-related functions.
  • Secure people are more likely to have secure partners and enduring, satisfying, and supportive relationships.

Antecedents of attachment (依附的前因): secure attachment is rooted in a history of sensitive (敏感的) and responsive interactions between caregiver and child.

  • Mothers of avoidant children tend to reject the infant or withdraw from contact.
  • Mothers of anxious-resistant children tend to be inconsistently or unreliably available (不穩定地可及).

Parenting interventions (親職介入) targeting attachment quality:

  • ABC (Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch-up; 依附與生物行為補救介入; Dozier & Bernard, 2017) — targets three caregiver behaviors: sensitivity to child's distress (對困擾的敏感性), following the child's lead, and reduced frightening behavior (e.g., yelling, grabbing). Outcomes cascade across infancy → early childhood → middle childhood: inhibitory control (抑制控制), emotion regulation (情緒調節), peer relations, reduced aggression, physiological regulation (生理調節).
  • VIPP-SD (Video-feedback Intervention to promote Positive Parenting and Sensitive Discipline; Juttfer et al., 2017) — uses video feedback to support sensitive parenting and firm limit-setting. Outcomes: fewer behavior problems, less disorganized attachment (混亂型依附), more joint attention, less autism-risk behaviors; effect on child autonomy with DRD4 7-repeat allele (重複等位基因) during mealtime.
Exam trap: Insecure-avoidant infants appear calm when the mother leaves — this is NOT a sign of healthy independence. It reflects suppression of distress and avoidance of contact, not secure exploration.

5. Intergenerational Transmission

Intergenerational transmission (代際傳遞) refers to the transfer of abilities, traits, behaviors, and outcomes from parents to children. Both parenting styles (Simons et al., 1991) and attachment patterns (Van IJzendoorn, 1997) show intergenerational transmission.

  • Multiple transmission mechanisms (傳遞機制): genetic transmission (基因傳遞), socio-economic environment (社會經濟環境), deliberate instruction (刻意教導), observational learning (觀察學習).
  • Child-to-parent transmission is also possible — transmission is bidirectional (雙向的).
  • Example of child-to-parent transmission: children exposed to inter-parental aggression learn to accept coercion and aggression as appropriate means to resolve conflicts, associating aggression with family roles (Ehrensaft et al., 2003; Stith et al., 2000).
  • Mothers' depression (母親抑鬱) contributes to her own life stress, adversely affects parenting quality and children's development, and adds stress to children — associated with children's depression in turn (Hammen et al., 2004).

6. Social Referencing and Parental Influence on Learning

Social referencing (社會參照) is the process by which an infant refers to another person's expressions to determine how to appraise a situation and respond to it.

  • Classic example (Klinnert, 1981): infants more likely to approach novel toys when mother smiled; avoided the toy when mother posed fear; showed mixed reactions to neutral expression.
  • Visual cliff experiment (視覺懸崖實驗; Sorce et al., 1985): when mother posed joy or interest, most infants crossed the deep side; when mother posed fear or anger, very few infants crossed.
  • Social referencing forms the basis of acquisition of anxiety (焦慮習得) and avoidance (Rapee, 2001).
  • Children had higher trait anxiety (特質焦慮) when parents were anxious, especially when parents often expressed their fears (Muris et al., 1996).

Parental math anxiety (父母數學焦慮) and homework help:

  • When parents were anxious about math (數學焦慮), children learned less over the school year and experienced more anxiety (Maloney et al., 2015).
  • Effect was particularly pronounced when anxious parents provided more homework help — during which they expressed negative attitudes and frustration, transmitting anxiety to children.

Parental mindset (父母心態) and praise style:

  • Fixed mindset parents ("ability is fixed" 能力是固定的) tend to give more person praise (人格稱讚; e.g., "you are smart").
  • Growth mindset parents ("ability is changeable" 能力可改變) tend to give more process praise (過程稱讚; e.g., "I like your effort").
  • These praise styles contribute to different learning orientations (mastery vs. performance 掌握目標 vs. 表現目標), levels of persistence (堅持性), and children's mindset (Gunderson et al., 2012; 2013).
Exam trap: More homework help from an anxious parent makes outcomes worse, not better. More help + parental anxiety = greater transmission of negative attitudes and more math anxiety in the child.
Exam Traps Summary
  • Authoritative vs. Authoritarian: Authoritative = high responsiveness + high demandingness (positive outcomes); Authoritarian = low responsiveness + high demandingness (negative outcomes). Do not confuse the two.
  • Psychological vs. Behavioral control: both are "control," but psychological control predicts worse outcomes while behavioral control predicts better outcomes.
  • Insecure-avoidant infants appear calm on separation — this is NOT healthy independence; it is avoidance and suppression of distress.
  • Authoritarian parenting is not universally maladaptive — in certain contexts (Asian societies, dangerous neighborhoods) its negative effects are reduced or absent.
  • More parental homework help is harmful, not helpful, when the parent is math-anxious — the additional interaction transmits the parent's anxiety and frustration.
  • Intergenerational transmission is not only parent-to-child; child-to-parent and bidirectional pathways also exist.
Top things to memorize
  1. Four parenting styles (Baumrind, 1991) and their placement on the responsiveness × demandingness grid.
  2. Authoritative parenting → best outcomes; authoritarian → unsociable/withdrawn; permissive → immature/low self-control; uninvolved → indifferent/rejecting.
  3. Psychological control (guilt, shame, love withdrawal) → worse outcomes; Behavioral control (rules, monitoring) → better outcomes.
  4. Ainsworth Strange Situation: three attachment types, their prevalence (~60% secure, ~20% avoidant, ~20% anxious-resistant), and reunion behaviors.
  5. Secure attachment predicts: sociability, friendship quality, emotional understanding, persistence, less psychopathology.
  6. Antecedents of attachment: avoidant ← rejecting/withdrawing mother; anxious-resistant ← inconsistently available mother.
  7. ABC intervention targets: sensitivity to distress, following child's lead, reduced frightening behavior.
  8. Cultural paradox (Chao, 1994): Chinese authoritarian parenting does not predict poor school outcomes as it does for European-Americans.
  9. Intergenerational transmission mechanisms: genetic, socio-economic, deliberate instruction, observational learning.
  10. Social referencing (Klinnert, 1981; Sorce et al., 1985): infants use caregiver expressions to appraise novel situations; basis of anxiety acquisition.
  11. Parental math anxiety × more homework help → worse child math outcomes (Maloney et al., 2015).
  12. Fixed mindset → person praise → performance orientation; Growth mindset → process praise → mastery orientation + persistence (Gunderson et al., 2012; 2013).

Lecture 6: What Exactly Happens During Psychotherapy?

Psychotherapy (心理治療) uses psychological techniques to treat disorders, resolve life problems, and improve quality of life. This lecture surveys the major therapy orientations — behavioural, cognitive, psychoanalytic, and person-centred — before examining what makes therapy effective and how digital technology is reshaping treatment delivery. The throughline is that effective therapy, regardless of orientation, shifts how clients think, feel, and behave.

1. Treating Psychological Disorders: Overview

Two main treatment categories exist for psychological disorders (心理障礙):

  • Psychotropic medications (精神科藥物) — drugs including antipsychotics (抗精神病藥), antidepressants (抗抑鬱藥), antianxiety agents (抗焦慮藥), and mood stabilizers (情緒穩定劑)
  • Psychotherapy (心理治療) — use of psychological techniques (心理技術) to treat psychological disorders, resolve people's problems in living, and improve life quality
Exam trap: Psychotherapy is NOT limited to treating disorders — it also addresses problems in living and life quality improvement. Do not confuse it with medication-only treatment.

2. Behavioural Approaches

Behavioural approaches (行為治療法) focus on specific problematic behaviours and build on basic processes of learning (學習過程) — the goal is to learn new, normal behaviour to replace maladaptive behaviour (適應不良行為).

  • Aversion therapy (厭惡療法) — pairs an unwanted behaviour with an unpleasant stimulus to reduce it
  • Token economy (代幣制度) — rewards desired behaviours with tokens exchangeable for privileges
  • Fearless peer (無懼同伴示範) — client observes a peer who shows no fear in a feared situation
  • Participant modeling (參與示範法) — therapist models a problematic situation and guides the client to cope with it unassisted; examples include assertion training (自我表達訓練) and behavioural rehearsal (行為演練)

Systematic Desensitization (系統脫敏法)

Clients are taught to relax as they are gradually exposed to what they fear in a stepwise manner — either imagined (想像暴露) or in vivo (實境暴露).

  • A structured anxiety hierarchy (焦慮層次表) is constructed, moving from mildest to most feared stimulus
  • Example hierarchy for dog phobia: looking at pictures → watching videos → observing from 100 ft → approaching at 50/25/10/5 ft → petting → playing → allowing the dog to lick
  • If the client reports anxiety at any point, the therapist interrupts the process and helps the client relax before continuing

Exposure Therapy (暴露療法)

A technique that confronts the client — either suddenly or gradually — with a feared stimulus (恐懼刺激).

  • Relaxation training (放鬆訓練) is omitted; the aim is to allow the anxiety or avoidance response (迴避反應) to extinguish (消退)
  • Response prevention (反應預防) — blocks the compulsive response, e.g., preventing handwashing for an OCD (強迫症) patient

3. Cognitive Approaches

Cognitive approaches (認知治療法) attempt to replace irrational thoughts (非理性思想) with more rational, adaptive thoughts (適應性思想). Sometimes called cognitive-behavioral therapy (認知行為治療, CBT) because they may also involve learning of adaptive behaviour.

  • Core assumption: anxiety (焦慮), depression (抑鬱), and other negative emotions stem from irrational thinking (e.g., "I will never be loved anymore")
  • Primary goal: reappraisal (重新評估) or cognitive restructuring (認知重組) — debunking irrational thoughts

Two Styles of Cognitive Restructuring

  • Confrontational style: therapist directly attacks dysfunctional beliefs (e.g., Martha case — "knock that out of your head")
  • Socratic style: therapist plays the role of teacher; client discovers disconfirming information on their own and discards irrational thinking (e.g., "My Husband Doesn't Love Me" case — client realises husband may simply be tired from work)

Cognitive Bias Modification (認知偏誤修正, CBM)

People vary in how they interpret ambiguous events (模糊事件) and whether they attend to negative or positive stimuli (刺激). CBM attempts to change maladaptive cognitive styles (適應不良認知風格) through systematically practising an alternative style.

  • A socially anxious person may focus attention on the only distracted face in a crowd; CBM trains a more adaptive attentional style
  • Example study (Dandeneau et al., 2007): participants trained to search for a smiling face in a matrix; compared to controls who searched for flowers, repeated training reduced rated work stress and led to lower cortisol levels (皮質醇水平)
  • Some game apps are developed based on CBM techniques
Exam trap: CBM is not just about changing thoughts — it targets automatic attentional biases (注意力偏誤) through repeated practice, and has measurable physiological effects (lower cortisol). Do not describe it as simple positive thinking.

  • Approaches combining multiple therapy orientations (治療取向)

5. Effectiveness of Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy as a whole is effective, but there is a significant treatment gap (治療缺口) — most people with a mental health condition receive no treatment.

  • Psychotherapy brings about greater improvement than does no treatment
  • Certain common factors (共同因素) are responsible for improvement across orientations: empathy (同理心), hope, new ways of thinking/feeling/behaving, connecting with others
  • Some approaches are more effective for specific disorders — e.g., CBT has a large effect on depression, generalized anxiety disorder (廣泛性焦慮症), panic disorder (恐慌症), social phobia (社交恐懼症), PTSD (創傷後壓力症), and childhood depressive and anxiety disorders (Butler et al., 2006)

Barriers to Treatment (治療障礙)

Two categories of barriers contribute to the treatment gap (Kazdin, 2023):

  • System factors (系統因素): cost of mental health services (費用), policy and legal constraints (政策與法律限制), too few providers to deliver services
  • Attitudinal factors (態度因素): mental health literacy (心理健康素養), stigma (汙名化), cultural and ethnic influences (文化與族裔影響)

6. Computer-Based and Digital Psychotherapy

Computer-based psychotherapy (電腦輔助心理治療) refers to therapy services or training delivered in a computer-based or technology-mediated manner (Price et al., 2014; Teachman, 2014). It is a sub-category of digital health (數位健康, APA, 2026).

  • In Veterans Health Administration (VHA), video tele-mental health visits increased eightfold in the decade before the COVID-19 pandemic (Rosen et al., 2021)
  • During the pandemic, VHA video tele-mental health visits increased by a large extent (Rosen et al., 2021) — face-to-face visits dropped sharply while video-to-home visits surged

App-Based and VR Applications

  • Mood tracking apps (情緒追蹤應用): therapists use apps (e.g., Mobile Mood Diary) to gather information about clients' mood, sleep quality, energy, and diary entries; also used to assign exercises (Matthews & Doherty, 2011)
  • CBM game apps (認知偏誤修正遊戲): apps based on the CBM technique, e.g., searching for smiling faces in a matrix
  • VR-based exposure therapy (虛擬實境暴露療法): examples include:
    • Anxiety — VR exposure to heights; learning effect: "Heights are safe"; outcome: realistic thoughts about heights
    • Eating disorders (飲食失調) — VR exposure to food; learning effect: "I can manage a healthy approach to food"; outcome: changes in thoughts about food
    • Paranoid beliefs (偏執信念) and social anxiety — VR social environment (e.g., restaurant scenario in Orygen Digital Revive study) exposes client to social interactions, crowdedness, and negative emotions from other customers

Exam Traps Summary
  • Psychotherapy treats disorders AND addresses problems in living and life quality — not limited to clinical disorders.
  • Systematic desensitization uses relaxation training; exposure therapy deliberately omits it. Response prevention belongs to exposure therapy.
  • CBM targets automatic attentional biases through repeated practice and has measurable physiological effects (lower cortisol) — not just positive thinking.
  • Computer-based psychotherapy is a sub-category of digital health, not the same thing; digital health is the broader umbrella.
  • Common factors (empathy, hope, new ways of thinking/feeling/behaving) contribute to therapy effectiveness across all orientations — not unique to any single approach.
  • CBT specifically has large effect sizes for depression, GAD, panic disorder, social phobia, PTSD, and childhood anxiety/depression (Butler et al., 2006) — not all approaches are equally effective for all disorders.
Top things to memorize
  1. Two treatment types: psychotropic medications vs. psychotherapy — and the four drug classes (antipsychotics, antidepressants, antianxiety agents, mood stabilizers)
  2. Systematic desensitization = gradual exposure + relaxation; exposure therapy = gradual or sudden exposure, NO relaxation, with response prevention
  3. Cognitive restructuring (reappraisal) is the primary goal of cognitive approaches; two styles — confrontational and Socratic/teacher-guided
  4. CBM changes maladaptive attentional/interpretive biases through repeated practice; Dandeneau et al. (2007) — smiling-face training reduced work stress and cortisol
  5. Psychoanalytic therapy: past-focused, prolonged, expensive; techniques = free association + dream analysis
  6. Person-centered therapy: present/future-focused; technique = unconditional positive regard; supportive environment for self-discovery
  7. Integrative/eclectic therapy: 13%–42% of therapists use this approach (Norcross & Goldfried, 2005)

Lecture 5: What Do Most People Get Wrong About Schizophrenia?

This lecture surveys the major categories of psychological disorders (心理疾病) — anxiety disorders, mood disorders, schizophrenia, obsessive-compulsive disorders, and personality disorders — with a focus on correcting the widespread misconception that schizophrenia equals a split personality. The lecture also examines the biological and cognitive causes of these disorders, including the dopamine hypothesis (多巴胺假說) and the diathesis-stress model (素質-壓力模型). Understanding prevalence rates, symptom clusters, and causal mechanisms is essential for distinguishing these disorders on exams.

1. Anxiety Disorders

Anxiety disorders (焦慮症) are characterized by distressing, persistent anxiety (焦慮) that causes impaired functioning and maladaptive behavior (適應不良行為) aimed at reducing anxiety. Some degree of anxiety is adaptive — it prepares people to cope with threats — but in anxiety disorders it is excessive. Global prevalence rate (全球盛行率): 4.4% (WHO, 2025).

Generalized Anxiety Disorder (廣泛性焦慮症, GAD):

  • General apprehension (憂慮) or excessive anxiety and worries, commonly about family, health, finances, work, or school problems
  • Often described as "free-floating anxiety" (漂浮性焦慮) — worry not tied to a specific trigger

Panic Disorder (恐慌症):

  • Unexpected (突如其來的) and frequent panic attacks (恐慌發作) not restricted to particular stimuli or situations, in the absence of specific danger
  • Panic attack (恐慌發作): wave of sudden, overwhelming fear with sense of losing control, feeling of unreality, extreme anxiety, and fear of imminent death (即將死亡的恐懼)
  • Attacks usually last several minutes; often occur multiple times per day or weekly
  • Physical symptoms: racing heart rate, chest pains, trouble breathing/hyperventilating, "pins and needles" in fingers or toes, choking feeling, trembling, sweating, numbness, lightheadedness, nausea
  • Psychological symptoms: feeling about to die or lose control, trouble concentrating, being easily startled or "on edge," fear of future panic attacks
Exam trap: A panic attack alone does not equal panic disorder. Panic disorder requires the attacks to be unexpected and recurring across situations — not tied to a specific known trigger (as in phobias).

Causes of Anxiety Disorders:

  • Cognitive perspective (認知觀點): Anxious people tend to catastrophize (災難化思維) — predicting terrible outcomes despite contrary evidence. Anxious individuals are more likely to interpret homophones as threatening words (Matthews & MacLeod, 2005)
  • Behavioral perspective (行為觀點): Phobias are acquired via classical conditioning (古典制約) — a neutral stimulus paired with an anxiety-arousing stimulus becomes feared; maintained via operant conditioning (操作制約) — avoidance reduces anxiety (negative reinforcement, 負強化), perpetuating the phobia

2. Mood Disorders

Mood disorders (情緒疾患) are disturbances in emotional experience (情緒體驗) strong enough to intrude on everyday living.

Major Depressive Disorder (重度憂鬱症, MDD):

  • Significantly depressed mood (憂鬱情緒) — feeling sad, irritable, or empty — often with decreased interest or pleasure in activities (anhedonia, 快感缺失)
  • Global prevalence rate: 4% overall; 5.7% among adults
  • Depressive episodes (憂鬱發作) last most of the day, nearly every day, for at least two weeks — distinct from regular mood fluctuations
  • Other symptoms: poor concentration, excessive guilt or low self-worth, hopelessness about the future, thoughts about dying or suicide, disrupted sleep, changes in appetite or weight, persistent low energy

Bipolar Disorder (躁鬱症):

  • Characterized by alternating manic (躁狂) and depressive episodes (憂鬱發作)
  • Global prevalence rate: 0.5%
  • Type I (第一型): one or more manic episodes interspaced with depressive episodes
  • Type II (第二型): one or more hypomanic (輕躁) episodes plus at least one depressive episode, but no full manic episode

Cognitive model of depression (Beck, 2008):

  • Adverse developmental experiences (早年不良經歷) lead to dysfunctional attitudes (功能失調的態度) — negative schemas (負向基模) about oneself, the world, and the future
  • When stressful events activate these schemas in later life, depression develops

3. Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia (思覺失調症) is characterized by significant impairments in the way reality (現實) is perceived and changes in behavior. Global prevalence rate: 0.29% overall; 0.43% among adults.

  • NOT to be confused with split personality (分裂人格), i.e., dissociative identity disorder (解離性身份疾患) — this is the most widespread public misconception
  • Causes significant distress and impairment in personal, family, and other life domains
  • Stigma (汙名化) is widespread, contributing to discrimination (歧視), which further impairs functioning
  • During humanitarian (人道主義) and public health emergencies, people with schizophrenia are more vulnerable than the general population

Core symptoms of schizophrenia:

  • Persistent delusions (妄想): fixed beliefs held despite evidence to the contrary
  • Persistent hallucinations (幻覺): hearing, smelling, seeing, touching, or feeling things that are not there
  • Experiences of influence, control, or passivity (被動體驗): feelings that one's thoughts, impulses, or actions are inserted, withdrawn, or broadcast by others
  • Disorganized thinking (思維混亂): often observed as jumbled or irrelevant speech
  • Highly disorganized behavior (高度混亂行為): bizarre or purposeless actions; unpredictable or inappropriate emotional responses
  • Negative symptoms (陰性症狀): very limited speech, restricted emotional expression, inability to experience interest or pleasure, social withdrawal
  • Catatonic symptoms (緊張症症狀): extreme agitation or slowing of movements, maintenance of unusual postures
  • Persistent cognitive difficulties: impaired memory (記憶), attention (注意力), and problem-solving (問題解決能力)
Exam trap: Schizophrenia does NOT mean "split personality." Split personality is dissociative identity disorder (解離性身份疾患), a completely separate condition. The split in schizophrenia refers to a split from reality (現實), not from one's own identity.

Causes of schizophrenia:

  • Genetic basis (遺傳基礎): Risk increases with genetic relatedness — identical twin of a person with schizophrenia has the highest risk (~48%); risk decreases for fraternal twins, children, siblings, and unrelated persons (Gottesman, 1991)
  • Dopamine hypothesis (多巴胺假說): schizophrenia results from excess activity in brain areas using dopamine (多巴胺) as a neurotransmitter (神經傳導物質); drugs that block dopamine action effectively reduce symptoms (Lieberman & Koreen, 1993)
  • Diathesis-stress model (素質-壓力模型): mental disorders are a joint product of genetic vulnerability (遺傳易感性) and environmental stressors (環境壓力) as triggers; early signs of vulnerability (e.g., social withdrawal, movement abnormalities) can appear well before full onset

Lecture 4: Is There More to a Good Life Than Happiness?

This lecture interrogates whether a good life can be reduced to happiness alone. It begins by defining and measuring subjective well-being (主觀幸福感), surveys the documented benefits of happiness, and explores the psychological causes of happiness. It then challenges the hedonic (享樂主義) view by introducing eudaimonic well-being (幸福論幸福感) and cultural variation in happiness ideals, arguing that meaning, purpose, and harmony may matter as much as pleasure.

1. Defining and Measuring Happiness

Subjective well-being (主觀幸福感, SWB) is the scientific concept for happiness — thinking and feeling that one's life is going very well. It focuses on life satisfaction (生活滿意度) and subjective pleasure (主觀愉悅感).

  • Positive feelings (正面情緒) — desirable, pleasant emotions people seek (e.g., enjoyment, love)
  • Negative feelings (負面情緒) — undesirable, unpleasant emotions people try to avoid (e.g., depression, anger, worry)
  • Life satisfaction (生活滿意度) — overall judgment of whether one's life is going well, by whatever standards the person finds most important (Diener, 2023)

Two main instruments measure SWB:

Instrument What it measures Scale Source
Scale of Positive and Negative Experience (SPANE) Frequency of positive/negative feelings over past 4 weeks (e.g., happy, sad, joyful, angry, contented) 1 (very rarely/never) to 5 (very often/always) Diener et al., 2009
Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) Global cognitive evaluation of life (5 items: closeness to ideal, excellent conditions, satisfaction, getting important things, would change nothing) 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) Diener et al., 1985
Cantril Ladder (Gallup World Poll) Life evaluation as a whole; respondents place themselves on a ladder from best (10) to worst (0) possible life 0–10 World Happiness Report
Exam trap: SWB has three distinct components — positive affect (正面情感), negative affect (負面情感), and life satisfaction (生活滿意度). Do not conflate these; a person can have high positive affect yet low life satisfaction.

2. Benefits of Happiness

Happy people (快樂的人) are healthier, more sociable, more productive, and better citizens (Diener, 2023). Key documented outcomes:

Domain (領域) Specific Benefits
Health and Longevity (健康與長壽) Stronger immune systems (免疫系統); fewer cardiovascular diseases (心血管疾病); more healthy behaviors (wearing seatbelts, medical adherence); longer average lifespan
Social Relationships (社交關係) More popular; relationships more stable and rewarding; divorced less; fired less; give and receive more social support
Productivity (生產力) Work units with higher SWB more productive; companies with happy workers earn more money and have higher stock prices
Citizenship (公民參與) More likely to donate time and money to charitable causes; more helpful behavior at work

Income and happiness exhibit a curvilinear (非線性) relationship: individuals with moderately high life satisfaction in 1984–1987 earned the highest incomes in 2000–2003, but beyond that peak, salary levels dropped (Oishi et al., 2007) — very high happiness does not maximize income.

The broaden-and-build theory (擴展與建構理論, Fredrickson, 2013) explains how positive emotions produce long-term benefits:

  • Broaden hypothesis (擴展假說) — positive emotions widen thoughts and motivations: creativity (創造力), flexibility (靈活性), openness to new information, action urges
  • Build hypothesis (建構假說) — broadening spurs development of enduring personal resources: resilience (韌性), social connection (社會連結), skills, knowledge
  • Upward spiral (向上螺旋) — built resources produce more positive emotions, which further broaden and build, enhancing health, survival, and fulfillment
Exam trap: The broaden-and-build theory has two distinct components. "Broaden" refers to immediate widening of thought repertoires (思維擴展); "build" refers to the long-term accumulation of resources (資源積累). Students often conflate them or omit the upward spiral mechanism.

3. Causes of Happiness

Happiness is determined by four main clusters of causes (Diener):

  • Income (收入) — related to happiness when income levels are low; above a moderate income level (approximately US$10,000 per capita GDP), additional income produces only small increases in SWB (correlation = .08; Diener & Seligman, 2004)
  • Life events (生活事件) — influence happiness only to a certain degree; adaptation (適應) dampens most effects over time
  • How we think (思維方式) — cognitive construal processes (認知建構過程) including social comparison (社會比較), savoring (品味), and gratitude (感恩) shape happiness
  • What we do (行為) — prosocial spending (親社會消費), volunteering, spending time in nature all raise happiness

Income–happiness relationship evidence:

  • Cross-national data: life satisfaction rises steeply with GDP per capita at low incomes; flattens above ~$10,000 (Diener & Biswas-Diener, 2002)
  • Japan and the US: despite large increases in economic prosperity from 1958 to 2000, average life satisfaction stayed remarkably constant (Diener & Seligman, 2004)

Adaptation (適應理論, hedonic adaptation):

  • People react to positive or negative life events, sometimes strongly, but reactions dampen over time
  • SWB tends to return toward its original baseline level (基線水平)
  • Evidence from 24,000 respondents (German Panel Study, Diener et al., 2006): adaptation observed for widowhood, divorce, unemployment, and marriage; the extent of adaptation varies across events

Construal theory of happiness (幸福建構理論, Lyubomirsky, 2001): objective circumstances affect happiness through multiple psychological processes including social comparison, savoring, and gratitude.

Gratitude journal experiment (Emmons et al., 2003): participants who wrote a weekly journal on gratitude (vs. hassles or neutral events) for 10 weeks reported better life satisfaction and fewer physical symptoms (生理症狀).

Prosocial spending (親社會消費) experiment (Dunn et al., 2008): participants given money to spend on others (donation or gift) reported higher subsequent happiness than those who spent on themselves.

Pro-environmental behavior (親環境行為) experiment (Prinzing, 2024): participants instructed to do three good things for the planet reported better SWB the next day compared to a control condition; fun condition showed similar gains.

Digital media and happiness: heavy use of social media, internet, and gaming is associated with lower happiness (Twenge & Farley, 2021); proposed mechanisms include displacement of beneficial activities (排擠有益活動), upward social comparison (向上社會比較), and cyberbullying (網絡欺凌). Effect is observed in both girls and boys.

Nature and happiness: frequency of visits to green spaces (綠地) and blue spaces (藍地, coastal and inland), and general nature connectedness (自然連結感), were positively associated with positive well-being across an 18-country study (White et al., 2021). A Hong Kong study of 2,800+ P4–P6 students found that stronger nature connectedness predicted lower likelihood of poor well-being (Ma et al., 2025).

Exam trap: Adaptation (適應) does not mean people return to exactly the same SWB level for all events — the extent varies. Do not claim adaptation is complete or universal; for some events (e.g., long-term unemployment) the return toward baseline is partial.

4. Is a Good Life Just a Happy Life?

Valuing happiness (重視幸福感) can be self-defeating (Mauss et al., 2011):

  • Among people with low life stress (低生活壓力), those who valued happiness more were less happy, less satisfied, and less flourishing than those who valued it less
  • They also reported more depression symptoms (抑鬱症狀)
  • Paradox: the goal of maximizing happiness can undermine the experience of it

Eudaimonic well-being (幸福論幸福感) — concept from Aristotle (亞里士多德): a good life involves doing what is worth doing, not merely what feels pleasant.

  • Distinct from hedonic pleasure (享樂愉悅): not all things worth doing produce hedonic pleasure
  • Ryff's model of eudaimonic well-being (六維模型, Ryff, 2023) identifies six components:
Component (成分) Description
Self-acceptance (自我接受) Positive attitude toward self; acknowledges and accepts multiple aspects of self
Positive relationships (正向關係) Warm, satisfying, trusting relationships with others
Autonomy (自主性) Self-determining and independent; evaluates self by personal standards
Environmental mastery (環境掌控感) Sense of mastery and competence in managing one's environment
Purpose in life (生命意義) Goals and directedness; feels meaning in present and past life
Personal growth (個人成長) Feeling of continued development; sees self as growing and expanding
Exam trap: Eudaimonic well-being (幸福論) and hedonic well-being (享樂論) are not the same thing. Hedonic = pleasure/happiness; eudaimonic = meaning, virtue, flourishing. A person can be eudaimonically well (pursuing meaningful goals) while experiencing low hedonic pleasure — and vice versa.

5. Cultural Variation in Happiness Ideals

Happiness maximization (幸福最大化) is a WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) phenomenon (Krys et al., 2024):

  • The idea that one should maximize happiness emerged particularly in Northwestern European cultures
  • The perceived ideal level (理想幸福水平) of happiness varies enormously across countries
  • Cross-national data show huge variation in how high people want their happiness to be

Happiness is not universally seen as the core of a good life (Krys et al., 2024). Different cultures emphasize different components:

Concept (概念) Key Elements Cultural Emphasis

Meaning (意義) Purpose; mattering; coherence (連貫感) Common across many cultures
Harmony (和諧) Relationship harmony; harmony with nature East Asian, collectivist cultures
Spirituality (靈性) Religiosity; appreciation of nature Many non-Western cultures
  • In some cultures, happiness (快樂) is just one of many components of a good life — not the primary goal
  • A Western happiness-centric model may not adequately capture well-being in non-WEIRD contexts
Exam Traps Summary
  • SWB has three distinct components — positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction — do not conflate them.
  • The broaden-and-build theory has two separate steps: broaden (immediate thought widening) and build (long-term resource accumulation); missing either step is an incomplete answer.
  • Adaptation to life events varies in extent — it is not complete or universal for all events; partial adaptation is common for unemployment.
  • Valuing happiness more can be self-defeating, especially under low life stress; this counterintuitive finding (Mauss et al., 2011) is a frequent exam focus.
  • Eudaimonic well-being is not the same as hedonic well-being; eudaimonic refers to meaning, virtue, and flourishing, not pleasure.
  • Happiness maximization is a WEIRD cultural phenomenon — it does not universally represent the ideal good life across all cultures.
  • The income–happiness relationship flattens above a moderate level (approx. US$10,000 GDP per capita); additional income at high levels produces minimal gains in SWB.
Top things to memorize

  2. Broaden-and-build theory (Fredrickson, 2013): broaden = widen thoughts; build = accumulate resources; upward spiral leads to health and fulfillment

  4. Income–happiness flattening: above US$10,000 GDP per capita, correlation drops to .08 (Diener & Seligman, 2004)

Lecture 3: Is Fearing Birds a Mental Disorder? Phobias and Mental Health

This lecture examines how society decides what counts as a mental disorder — a surprisingly contested question. Starting from the core criteria of deviance, distress, and dysfunction, it traces how diagnostic systems like the DSM and ICD operationalise those criteria, how cultural context and historical change shift the boundary between normal and abnormal, and how biological, psychological, and social forces interact to produce mental illness. Understanding these frameworks is essential for critically evaluating any diagnosis, including phobias.

1. Defining Mental Illness: Key Criteria

Mental illness (精神疾病) — also called mental disorder (精神障礙), psychological disorder (心理障礙), or psychiatric disorder (精神科障礙) — is a fuzzy concept (模糊概念). No single criterion (標準) can define it; several criteria are relevant:

  • Deviance (偏差) / statistical rarity (統計稀有性) — some disorders are rare (e.g., schizophrenia 思覺失調症), but others are not; WHO estimates that in 2025, 1 in every 7 people (over 1 billion) live with a mental disorder. Also, some rare conditions (e.g., exceptional athletic ability, creativity) are not considered illnesses.
  • Distress (心理痛苦) — many disorders (e.g., depression 抑鬱症) produce intensive distress, but some (e.g., personality disorders 人格障礙) typically do not.
  • Dysfunctional / Impairment (功能損害) — most disorders impair everyday functioning (job, marriage, relationships); however, conditions like rudeness or laziness also impair functioning without being disorders.
Exam trap: Statistical rarity alone does NOT define mental illness — exceptional abilities like creativity are rare but not disorders. All three criteria (deviance, distress, dysfunction) must be considered together, not individually.

Cultural Variations

Cultural context (文化背景) complicates classification. Culture-bound syndromes (文化特定症候群) are mental symptoms or conditions specific to one or a limited number of societies.

  • Koro (縮陽症) — sudden, intense anxiety that one's genitals are retracting into the body and may cause death; found primarily in Southeast Asia and parts of Africa; reported in mass form in Assam/Bengal, India (1982, thousands of cases). Not classified as a formal disorder but as a culture-bound syndrome.
  • Hikikomori (繭居族) — severe social withdrawal (社交退縮); over 1 million cases in Japan, initially considered a cultural syndrome; similar cases have since been identified outside Japan (Kato et al., 2012), raising questions about its culture-bound status.

Changes Over Time

Some conditions once classified as mental disorders are now considered normal, and vice versa.

  • Homosexuality (同性戀) — classified as a mental disorder by the APA and WHO; removed from the APA diagnostic manual in 1973; WHO followed in 1990.
  • Romantic relationships with fictional/AI characters — increasingly reported in Japan and other countries; debated whether it constitutes a mental illness.
Exam trap: Homosexuality was removed from the APA diagnostic manual in 1973 — not by the WHO. The WHO removal came later, in 1990. Do not conflate the two dates.

2. Diagnosis and Diagnostic Systems

Diagnosis (診斷) serves three functions:

  • Pinpointing the problem a person is experiencing
  • Informing the design of treatment plans (治療計劃)
  • Facilitating communication among mental health professionals (精神健康專業人員)

Two major diagnostic systems exist:

Feature DSM-5 ICD-11
Publisher American Psychiatric Association (APA) World Health Organization (WHO)
Number of disorders 237 disorders Broader; includes all diseases

Overlap Categories largely similar to ICD Categories largely similar to DSM; some differences

DSM-5 chapters include:

  • Neurodevelopmental Disorders (神經發展障礙)
  • Schizophrenia Spectrum and Other Psychotic Disorders
  • Bipolar and Related Disorders (雙相情緒障礙)
  • Depressive Disorders; Anxiety Disorders (焦慮症)
  • Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders (強迫症); Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders
  • Dissociative Disorders; Somatic Symptom Disorders; Feeding and Eating Disorders
  • Elimination Disorders; Sleep-Wake Disorders; Sexual Dysfunctions
  • Gender Dysphoria; Disruptive, Impulse Control and Conduct Disorders
  • Substance Use and Addictive Disorders; Neurocognitive Disorders
  • Personality Disorders; Paraphilic Disorders; Other Disorders

Diagnosis is made by a mental health professional (e.g., psychiatrist 精神科醫師, psychologist 心理學家) based on: clinical interview (臨床訪談), physical examination, psychological testing, and diagnostic criteria (診斷標準).

Example: Major Depressive Disorder (重度抑鬱症 / MDD) criteria — 5 or more of the following 9 symptoms (must include at least 1 of the first two) persisting in the same 2-week period, each representing a change from previous functioning:

  1. Depressed mood (subjective or observed)
  2. Loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia 快感缺失)
  3. Change in weight or appetite
  4. Insomnia (失眠) or hypersomnia (嗜睡)
  5. Psychomotor retardation (精神運動遲緩) or agitation (observed)
  6. Loss of energy or fatigue
  7. Worthlessness or guilt (內疚)
  8. Impaired concentration or indecisiveness
  9. Thoughts of death or suicidal ideation (自殺意念) or suicide attempt

Comorbidity (共病性) — co-occurrence of two mental disorders — is common. DSM-5 data: 41% of people with OCD (強迫症) also meet criteria for MDD; over half of people with a primary depressive disorder also exhibit some anxiety disorder (焦慮症), and the reverse is also true.

Exam trap: For MDD, you need 5 or more symptoms — but the 5 must include at least 1 of "depressed mood" OR "loss of interest/pleasure." Simply having 5 symptoms from the other 7 is insufficient for diagnosis.

3. Medicalization and the Expanding Diagnostic Boundary

Medicalization (醫療化) refers to the growing number of diagnoses in the DSM and the increasing number of people on medications for psychological problems (Barber, 2008). There is concern that mild and normal disturbances are too easily classified as pathological (Frances & Widiger, 2012).

  • The DSM has grown substantially with each edition, expanding the range of behaviours classified as disorders.
  • Example controversy: DSM-5 removed the "bereavement exclusion (哀傷排除條款)" — individuals can now be diagnosed with MDD following loss of a loved one (e.g., death of a spouse) if they meet diagnostic criteria, where previous editions encouraged excluding bereavement first (The Lancet Editorial, 2012; Lilienfeld et al., 2015).
  • Key question: Is grief (哀傷) a normal human response or a symptom of pathology (病理)? The boundary between normal sadness and clinical depression is contested.
Exam trap: DSM-5 (not earlier editions) removed the bereavement exclusion, allowing grief after loss to qualify for a MDD diagnosis. This is the change that drew criticism — earlier DSMs protected against this classification.

4. Explaining Mental Illness: Historical and Contemporary Theories

Supernatural theories (超自然理論) — dominant in the Middle Ages and earlier, odd behaviors and psychotic experiences were attributed to possession by evil or demonic spirits (惡靈). Treatments included:

  • Religious ceremonies (宗教儀式), exorcisms (驅魔), witch-hunting (獵巫)
  • Trephination (顱骨穿孔術) — drilling holes in the skull to release evil spirits trapped within; one of the oldest known surgical procedures

Somatogenic theories (軀體成因理論) — mental illness attributed to disturbances in physical functioning from illness, genetic inheritance (遺傳), or brain damage/imbalance.

  • Hippocrates (460–370 BC) — believed mental illness was caused by excess or deficiency of four essential bodily fluids (體液): blood (血液), yellow bile (黃膽汁), black bile (黑膽汁), and phlegm (痰液)
  • Treatment example: blood-letting (放血) for someone too temperamental (attributed to excess blood)

Psychogenic theories (心因性理論) — mental illness results from traumatic or stressful experiences (創傷/壓力) and maladaptive cognitions (適應不良的認知) and behaviors. Treatments developed include:

  • Compassionate care (人道護理), psychoanalysis (精神分析), behavioral therapy (行為治療), cognitive therapy (認知治療)

The biopsychosocial approach (生理心理社會模式) — currently dominant; considers psychological disorders as an outcome of the interaction between biological, psychological, and socio-cultural factors:

Domain Examples
Biological (生理) Evolution, individual genes, brain structure and chemistry
Psychological (心理) Stress, trauma, learned helplessness (習得性無助), mood-related perceptions and memories
Social-Cultural (社會文化) Roles, expectations, definitions of normality and disorder
  • Past experience, irrational thoughts (非理性思想), and family dynamics (家庭動態) are now considered in diagnosis and treatment.

5. Risk Factors and Protective Factors

Risk factors (風險因素) for mental illness operate at individual and environmental levels:

  • Individual factors: substance use (物質使用), genetics (遺傳因素), poor emotional skills (情緒技能不足)
  • Social and environmental factors: poverty, violence, inequality, pollution, economic downturns, outbreaks, humanitarian emergencies, wars and conflicts
  • Sensitive periods (敏感期): risks occurring during early childhood are particularly harmful — e.g., bullying (校園欺凌), harsh parenting (嚴苛管教)

Protective factors (保護因素) reduce the likelihood or severity of mental disorders:

  • Individual factors: social skills (社交技能), positive social interactions (正向社交互動)
  • Social and environmental factors: access to quality education, decent work (體面工作), safe neighborhoods, community ties (社區連結)
  • No single risk or protective factor can reliably and singlehandedly explain mental health outcomes.

6. Responding to the Mental Health Crisis: WHO Framework

A wholistic approach (整全方法) to battling the mental health crisis is required (WHO, 2022). The framework has four pillars:

Pillar Key Actions
Deepen Value and Commitment (深化重視與承擔) Understand intrinsic value (內在價值) of mental health; promote social inclusion (社會共融) of people with mental health conditions; intensify engagement across sectors; step up investment
Reshape Environments (重塑環境) Reshape physical, social, and economic characteristics of homes, schools, workplaces, health care services, communities, and natural environments
Strengthen Mental Health Care (強化精神健康照護) Build community-based networks of services; move away from custodial care (院舍式護理) in psychiatric hospitals; diversify and scale up care options; make mental health affordable and accessible
Value Transformation (價值轉化) Promote person-centred (以人為本), human rights-based (人權為本) care; give mental and physical health equal priority; engage and empower people with lived experience (親歷者)
  • The framework explicitly calls for moving away from custodial (institutional) psychiatric care toward community-based, rights-based models.
  • People with lived experience (親身經歷者) of mental illness should be engaged and empowered in shaping services.
Exam Traps Summary

  • Homosexuality was removed from the APA manual in 1973; the WHO followed in 1990 — do not conflate the two dates.
  • For MDD diagnosis: 5+ symptoms are needed, but at least 1 must be either "depressed mood" or "loss of interest/pleasure."
  • DSM-5 (not earlier editions) removed the bereavement exclusion, allowing grief after loss to qualify as MDD if criteria are met.
  • Koro and hikikomori: Koro remains a culture-bound syndrome; hikikomori was initially culture-bound but cases have been found outside Japan.
  • Comorbidity is common — 41% of OCD patients also meet MDD criteria; over half of MDD patients show some anxiety disorder.
  • The biopsychosocial model replaced earlier single-cause theories; no single biological, psychological, or social factor fully explains mental disorders.

Lecture 2: Are You More Introverted or Extraverted?

This lecture examines how psychologists describe and measure personality (人格), moving from the foundational trait approach through the Big Five and HEXACO models to practical assessment tools. The central question is whether personality is best captured by continuous dimensions or discrete types, and how different measurement methods compare in reliability and validity. Real-world applications — from hiring to targeted advertising — show why the answer matters.

1. Personality and Trait Theory

  • Personality (人格) — enduring predispositions (持久傾向) that characterize a person, including styles of thought (思維方式), feelings, and behavior; tend to vary across individuals
  • Trait theories (特質理論) — identify the structure of personality and the basic traits (特質) necessary to describe it; do not explain what causes personality
  • Trait dimensions are continuous: characteristics range from low to high, with intermediate values; comparison and description across individuals are possible

Factor analysis (因素分析) — statistical method that identifies general patterns underlying associations among a large number of variables

  • Researchers input many personality variables (e.g., sociability, liveliness, risk-taking, impulsivity)
  • The method reveals which variables cluster together, identifying the underlying basic traits
  • Applied to trait terms in dictionaries and literature, factor analysis consistently reveals a five-factor structure

Exam trap: Trait theories describe and compare personality but do NOT explain what causes personality — do not conflate description with causal explanation.

2. The Big Five Model (OCEAN)

  • Big Five (五大人格) — five broad traits that summarize much of the variability in human personality
  • Derived from factor analysis of trait terms; observed consistently across methods, populations, and cultures (文化)
  • Acronym: OCEAN — Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism

Trait Low Scorer High Scorer Dimension Labels
Openness (開放性) Narrow interests; inartistic; down-to-earth; prefers not to be exposed to alternative moral systems Imaginative; untraditional; enjoys new ideas; creative Independent–Conforming; Imaginative–Practical; Variety–Routine
Conscientiousness (盡責性) Disorganized; unreliable; hedonistic; careless; lax Organized; hardworking; neat; persevering; punctual; self-disciplined Careful–Careless; Disciplined–Impulsive
Extraversion (外向性) Prefers quiet evenings; sober; aloof; unenthusiastic Life of the party; active; optimistic; fun-loving; affectionate Sociable–Retiring; Fun-loving–Sober
Agreeableness (親和性) Quick to assert own rights; irritable; manipulative; uncooperative; rude Good-natured; forgiving; helpful; agrees with others; gullible Kind–Cold; Appreciative–Unfriendly
Neuroticism (神經質) / Emotional Stability Calm; unemotional; hardy; secure; self-satisfied Worries about little things; insecure; hypochondriacal; feeling inadequate; anxious Stable–Tense; Calm–Anxious; Secure–Insecure

Real-world correlates of Big Five traits (empirical findings):

  • High conscientiousness, low neuroticism, high agreeableness → better job performance (Tett et al., 1991)
  • Extraversion (外向性) particularly important for salesperson (銷售人員) performance (Furnham & Fudge, 2008)
  • Openness → preference for reflective and complex music (e.g., classical, jazz); Extraversion + Agreeableness → energetic and rhythmic music (e.g., hip-hop, funk) (Rentfrow & Gosling, 2003)
  • Openness and Neuroticism predicted higher rates of sheltering-in-place (居家隔離) during COVID-19; Extraversion negatively related to staying at home (Gotz et al., 2021)
  • Analysis of 700 million words from 75,000 Facebook users: Big Five traits predict language use on Facebook (Schwartz et al., 2013)
  • Personality-matched ads in 3.5 million Facebook users: congruent (一致的) ads resulted in up to 40% more clicks and up to 50% more purchases compared to non-personalized ads (Matz et al., 2017)

Exam trap: Extraversion is negatively related to sheltering-in-place during COVID-19 (more extraverted = less likely to stay home), while Openness and Neuroticism are positively related — do not reverse these directions.

3. HEXACO Model

  • HEXACO — six-trait model (六特質模型); five traits are variants of the Big Five (emotionality, extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, openness)
  • Sixth trait: Honesty-Humility (誠實謙遜) — high scorers are sincere and modest; low scorers are self-centered (自我中心), manipulative (操縱性), and narcissistic (自戀的)
  • Honesty-Humility is relevant to ethical (道德), materialistic (物質主義), and power-seeking behavior
  • Honesty-Humility shows the strongest negative relation with workplace deviance (職場偏差行為) — e.g., stealing, damaging company property, intentionally working slowly, gossiping, harassing coworkers — out of all HEXACO traits (Pletzer et al., 2019)

Exam trap: HEXACO has 6 traits, not 5. The unique addition is Honesty-Humility — not a sixth variant of an existing Big Five trait. Low Honesty-Humility predicts workplace deviance more strongly than any other trait.

4. Personality Tests: Types and Evaluation

Criteria for evaluating any personality test (人格測驗):

  • Reliability (信度) — consistency of responses across items and across repeated assessments
  • Validity (效度) — extent to which a measure assesses what it purports to measure; criterion validity (效標效度) means the test predicts trait-related behavior
  • Both require empirical data (實證數據) to establish
  • Personality/behavioral assessments are used in hiring: most often for high-level positions (executives > middle managers > individual contributors > hourly workers); about one third of HR specialists used personality tests when hiring for executive positions (SHRM, 2017)

Self-report tests (自陳量表)

  • Respondents answer questions using a limited set of options; responses are scored and interpreted in a standardized manner (標準化方式)
  • Possible to test reliability and validity
  • Threats to validity (效度威脅): inaccurate or inaccessible self-concept (自我概念); biases including social desirability (社會期許偏誤) and self-enhancement (自我提升偏誤)

Projective tests (投射測驗)

  • Test-taker interprets ambiguous stimuli (模糊刺激); assumption: important thoughts, feelings, and motives operate outside conscious awareness (意識之外)
  • Projective hypothesis (Frank, 1949): designed to obtain what the test taker "cannot or will not say, frequently because he does not know himself"
  • Rorschach Test (羅夏克墨跡測驗) — series of symmetrical inkblots (對稱墨跡); test-takers describe what each inkblot looks like
  • Thematic Apperception Test (主題統覺測驗, TAT) — test-takers make up a dramatic story for each picture, including what led up to the event, what is happening, what characters feel, and the outcome
  • Threats to reliability (信度威脅): responses could be inconsistent; interpretation of responses could be subjective (主觀的)
  • Threats to validity: characteristics to be measured not always clearly defined; responses may be limited by other traits (e.g., verbal ability, imagination)

Test Type Method Reliability Validity Key Threats
Self-report (自陳量表) Standardized questionnaire items Testable Testable (criterion validity) Social desirability, self-enhancement, inaccessible self-concept
Projective (投射測驗) Ambiguous stimuli interpretation Difficult; subjective scoring Difficult; constructs often undefined Subjectivity, verbal ability, imagination confounds

Automatic assessment (自動化評估) — computer-based assessment of personality via digital footprint (數位足跡) analysis (Park et al., 2015)

  • With ~90–100 Facebook Likes, a computer model matches the accuracy of the average human judge (r = 0.49) (Wu et al., 2015)
  • Computer average accuracy: r = 0.56; humans average: r = 0.49; spouse: r = 0.58; cohabitant: r = 0.45; family: r = 0.50; work colleague: r = 0.27
  • Facebook Likes are most predictive for Openness and Conscientiousness; Facebook statuses contribute additional predictive accuracy for multiple traits (Gladstone et al., 2019)
  • Spending records (消費記錄), Flickr pictures, and music preferences also predict Big Five traits with varying accuracy

Exam trap: The computer model does NOT exceed the accuracy of a spouse (r = 0.58) overall — it matches the average human (r = 0.49) at ~90–100 Likes. Do not claim computers outperform all human raters.

5. Personality Types vs. Dimensions

Personality types (人格類型) — classification of individuals into distinct groups (e.g., MBTI, Enneagram)

  • Imply relative homogeneity (同質性) within a group and heterogeneity (異質性) between groups

Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI, 邁爾斯-布里格斯類型指標) — four dichotomies (二元對立), producing 16 types:

  • E/I — Extraversion vs. Introversion: outwardly focused, talkative, fast-paced vs. inwardly focused, reserved, contemplative
  • S/N — Sensing vs. Intuition (直覺): focus on concrete facts and details, practical applications vs. imagine possibilities, notice big picture, enjoy ideas for their own sake
  • T/F — Thinking vs. Feeling: decisions via impersonal logical reasoning vs. decisions based on personal values and impact on others
  • J/P — Judging vs. Perceiving: prefers matters settled, respects rules and deadlines, makes plans vs. prefers open options, sees rules as flexible, spontaneous

Limitations of MBTI and personality type approaches:

  • MBTI is popular but has limited quality evidence for reliability and validity; systematic review (Randall et al., 2017) found only 7 studies with sufficient information, variable quality, 6 of which used only student samples
  • Personality variations fall on a bell curve (常態分布), not distinct categories (Pittenger, 2005) — lack of bimodality (雙峰分布), high frequency of scores at scale midpoints
  • The four-letter type formula creates the impression of meaningful difference between profiles when no such difference exists (Pittenger, 2005)
  • Personality type classification lacks scientific rigor (科學嚴謹性)

Exam trap: Type models (like MBTI) imply distinct categories separated by meaningful boundaries, but empirical data show a bell curve — most people score near the midpoint, not at the extremes. Types impose false categorical boundaries on continuous variation.
Exam Traps Summary
  • Trait theories describe and compare personality but do NOT explain what causes it — description is not causation.
  • Extraversion is negatively related to COVID-19 sheltering-in-place; Openness and Neuroticism are positively related — do not reverse the directions.
  • HEXACO has 6 traits: Honesty-Humility is the unique addition; low Honesty-Humility (not low Agreeableness) is the strongest predictor of workplace deviance.
  • The computer model with ~90–100 Facebook Likes matches the average human judge (r = 0.49), not the spouse (r = 0.58) — computers do not outperform all human raters.
  • MBTI personality types lack scientific rigor: extraversion scores follow a bell curve (most near midpoint), not a bimodal distribution — the four-letter formula creates the illusion of meaningful categorical differences that do not actually exist.
Top things to memorize

  2. HEXACO = Big Five + Honesty-Humility; low Honesty-Humility predicts workplace deviance most strongly (Pletzer et al., 2019)
  3. Factor analysis identifies underlying patterns from large variable sets — basis for deriving the Big Five
  4. Reliability = consistency across items and time; Validity = measures what it claims to measure (criterion validity = predicts trait-related behavior)
  5. Self-report threats: social desirability bias, self-enhancement bias, inaccessible self-concept
  6. Projective tests (Rorschach, TAT): assume unconscious motives; threats = subjective scoring, confounds from verbal ability/imagination
  7. Automatic assessment: ~90–100 Facebook Likes matches average human accuracy (r = 0.49); spouse accuracy r = 0.58
  8. Personality-matched ads: up to 40% more clicks, up to 50% more purchases (Matz et al., 2017)

Lecture 1: Lecture 1: Introduction to Psychology and Society

This lecture introduces the Psychology of AI (人工智能心理學) as an interdisciplinary field that bridges cognitive psychology, human factors engineering, human-computer interaction, and neuroscience. It examines how people perceive, trust, and collaborate with AI systems, and how AI use affects cognition, productivity, and wellbeing. The central throughline is that AI does not merely change what we do but reshapes how we think, feel, and relate to technology and each other.

1. What Is Psychology of AI

  • Psychology of AI (人工智能心理學) — bridges areas within psychology and disciplines outside psychology, including cognitive psychology (認知心理學), human factors engineering (人因工程), human-computer interaction (人機互動), and neuroscience (神經科學)
  • Growing urgency (迫切性) — AI is embedded in healthcare (醫療), education, work, and everyday life
  • Core topics examined: trust (信任), emotion (情緒), cognition (認知), productivity (生產力), and social behaviour (社會行為) in AI contexts

  • Future topics within the subject: AI tools in facilitating research, how humans think and AI development, AI applications in therapeutic and mental health settings (digital mental health 數位心理健康), ethics in AI use (AI倫理), and relationships with AI (AI chatbots 聊天機器人, AI companions 人工智能伴侶)

2. Trust in AI

  • Trust in AI (對人工智能的信任) predicts acceptance of AI and intention to use AI (Choung et al., 2023)
  • Successful integration of AI into organisations depends on workers' trust in AI (Glickson & Woolley, 2021)
  • Trust in AI-assisted health systems shapes people's use (Sagona et al., 2025)

Trustworthiness (可信度), trust (信任), and trusting behaviour (信任行為) are distinct from each other. Trust in AI can be understood as a psychological process (心理過程) from objective trustworthiness of a system to users' trusting use of the system (Everett et al., 2026).

Stage Question Nature
Actual trustworthiness (實際可信度) Does this system actually deserve users' trust? Objective attribute (客觀屬性)
Impression formation (印象形成) Do I think this system deserves users' trust? Propensity to trust (信任傾向)
Intention to rely (依賴意圖) Am I willing to rely on this system? Perceived risks and stakes (感知風險)
Trusting use (信任使用) Will I actually rely on this system in practice? Actual behaviour (實際行為)

Trust in AI is multidimensional (多維度的); the framework identifies three constituting components (Bedue & Fritzsche, 2021):

  • Ability (能力) — access to knowledge, system and service quality
  • Integrity (誠信) — standards and guidelines, ethical behaviour, government regulation
  • Benevolence (善意) — social responsibility, sustainability
Additional factors feeding into trust: transparency (透明度), data quality (數據質量), certifications (認證), explainability (可解釋性), reliability (可靠性), perceived benefits (感知利益), perceived risks (感知風險), and intention to use (使用意圖).

An integrative framework (Li et al., 2024) identifies three categories of trust determinants:

  • Trustor (信任者) factors — demographics (人口特徵), emotional experience (情緒體驗), familiarity (熟悉度), hedonic motivation (享樂動機), self-efficacy (自我效能感), sense of control (控制感), trust propensity (信任傾向)
  • Trustee (被信任者) factors — accountability (問責性), anthropomorphism (擬人化), competence (能力), explainability, fairness (公平性), privacy protection (隱私保護), robustness (穩健性), transparency, warmth (溫暖感)
  • Interactive context (互動情境) — culture (文化), facilitating conditions (促進條件), perceived benefits, perceived uncertainties (感知不確定性), safeguards (保障措施), social influence (社會影響)

Exam trap: Trustworthiness, trust, and trusting behaviour are NOT the same thing. Trustworthiness is an objective attribute of the system; trust is a psychological state of the user; trusting behaviour is the actual act of relying on the system. Do not conflate them.

3. AI as Social Actors

  • Perceptions of AI are more than assessment of its ability and performance
  • Unlike other technologies, humans tend to think of AI as social actors (社會行動者) (McKee et al., 2023)
  • Humans assess AI's warmth (溫暖感) — friendliness (友善), sincerity (真誠), and empathy (同理心)
  • Humans assess AI's competence (能力) — intelligence (智慧), power, and efficiency (效率)
  • Humans assess AI's mental abilities (心智能力) — intelligence and sentience (感知力)

  • Different AI systems elicit different warmth/competence profiles — virtual assistants and smart vacuums are perceived as warmer; chess-playing systems and video recommenders as more competent (McKee et al., 2023)
  • People perceive AI systems that pursue interests aligned with their own as warmer; systems that operate independently from human oversight as more competent
  • In a China study (Li et al., 2025), warmth and competence were the two most influential factors affecting trust in AI; other significant factors: anthropomorphism (擬人化), robustness, social influence, and participant characteristics

  • Researchers collected over 12,000 metaphor (隱喻) responses over a year from a U.S. sample (Cheng et al., 2026)
  • Participants perceived AI as warm and competent; attributions of human-likeness (人類相似性) and warmth increased significantly in the year after ChatGPT was introduced
  • Increasing dominant metaphors (r > 0.3): assistant, friend, robot, child, teacher, god
  • Decreasing dominant metaphors (r < -0.3): search engine, computer, mirror, synthesizer
  • Warmth and competence perceptions strongly predicted trust and willingness to use AI; metaphoric narratives (隱喻敘述) about AI and perception of anthropomorphism also predicted trust and use willingness

Exam trap: Warmth and competence are independent dimensions — an AI can score high on one and low on the other. Do not assume a competent AI is also perceived as warm.

4. Human-AI Collaboration and the Performance Paradox

Performance paradox (表現悖論) — AI can improve immediate outcomes while potentially reducing critical thinking (批判性思維), metacognitive monitoring (後設認知監控), skill development (技能發展), and later unaided performance (無輔助表現).

Productivity gains (生產力提升) from AI assistants:

  • Call centre workers (客服中心工作者) using AI-based conversational assistants resolved 15% more issues (Brynyjolfsson et al., 2025)
  • Field experiments at Microsoft and Accenture found a 26.8% boost in productivity among software developers (Cui et al., 2026)
  • In a study with realistic professional tasks (press releases, short reports, delicate emails), the ChatGPT-enabled group completed tasks 40% faster and output quality rose 18% (Noy & Zhang, 2023); treatment effect: -0.75 SDs on time taken, +0.41 SDs on grade
  • ChatGPT has potential to level productivity inequality (生產力不平等): participants with lower initial grades benefited more from ChatGPT access (Noy & Zhang, 2023)

  • Cognitive offloading (認知卸載) — delegation of cognitive tasks to external resources (e.g., AI) in order to reduce mental demand (心理負荷)
  • Ideally, AI frees resources for higher-level thinking (高階思維), augmenting performance
  • A cross-sectional study (Gerlich, 2025) found a significant negative correlation between frequent AI tool use and critical thinking abilities (r = -0.68), mediated by increased cognitive offloading; AI tool use correlated positively with cognitive offloading (r = +0.72); cognitive offloading correlated negatively with critical thinking (r = -0.75)

  • Automation bias (自動化偏誤) — tendency to over-rely on automated systems and trust computer-generated advice over personal judgement (個人判斷)
  • Users perceive AI as more objective (客觀), reliable, and intelligent than humans, causing them to cease verification (核實) and independent thinking
  • When AI errs, this bias degrades decision quality (決策質量) — a critical risk in high-stakes domains (高風險領域)

  • Skill atrophy (技能萎縮) — prolonged AI assistance may reduce proficiency (熟練度) in delegated tasks
  • AI use may impair intrinsic motivation (內在動機) in work
  • An experiment on programming library mastery (Shen & Tamkin, 2026) found AI use caused significant decrease in skills; skill development depended on usage patterns (認知參與程度 cognitively engaging or not)
  • Six AI interaction personas identified: Generation-Then-Comprehension (86% quiz score, 24 min), Hybrid Code-Explanation, Conceptual Inquiry, AI Delegation (39% quiz score), Progressive AI Reliance (35%), Iterative AI Debugging (31%, 31 min)

GenAI (生成式人工智能) impact on motivation and performance persistence (Wu et al., 2025):

  • GenAI enhanced immediate task performance but augmentation (增強效果) did not persist in subsequent solo tasks
  • Shifting from working with GenAI to solo work accompanied by larger decrease in intrinsic motivation and larger increase in boredom (無聊感)
  • Tasks studied across three experiments: composing a Facebook post, writing a performance review, drafting a welcoming email (paired with AI collaboration), and alternative use test, product improvement task, product promotion task (solo follow-ups)

Implications and mitigation (緩解措施):

  • Designers of AI tools must balance immediate performance gains against long-term costs to learning and development
  • Users' AI literacy (人工智能素養) and metacognition (後設認知) are important during collaboration with AI
  • Collaborative AI literacy — competencies that enable individuals to critically evaluate (批判性評估) collaborative AI tools, communicate and coordinate with them effectively, and use them well (Sidra & Mason, 2026)
  • Collaborative AI metacognition — the ability to enhance awareness and control of one's thinking process when working with AI tools, through planning (計劃), monitoring (監控), and reflection (反思) (Sidra & Mason, 2026)

Exam trap: Cognitive offloading is not inherently negative — it is only harmful when it replaces rather than supplements higher-level thinking. Automation bias is the problematic variant where verification and independent judgement are abandoned entirely.

5. AI Anxiety

AI anxiety (人工智能焦慮) is not uniform; it is a cluster of worries and apprehensions about job loss (工作流失), skill obsolescence (技能過時), privacy (私隱), opacity (不透明性), and loss of control (失控感) due to the development of AI.

Dimension (維度) Example statement
Privacy violation (侵犯私隱) I'm afraid that AI will monitor my behaviour
Bias behaviour (偏見行為) AI treats different people differently, which can make me anxious
Job replacement (工作替代) I am worried that AI will replace my work in the future
Learning (學習困難) AI technology updates too quickly and is very difficult to learn
Existential risk (存在風險) AI may harm humans to achieve a goal, which gives me anxiety
Against ethics (違反倫理) I worry that humans have special feelings (e.g. love or adoration) for super AI
Artificial consciousness (人工意識) I worry that AI will attain the same level of consciousness as humans
Lack of transparency (缺乏透明度) It's worrying if you do not know which part of AI has erred after AI makes a mistake

People's attitudes toward AI are likely to be ambivalent (矛盾的) (Noy & Zhang, 2023):

  • Participants who used ChatGPT enjoyed tasks more than control participants
  • Their concern for job replacement was higher (+0.266 SDs, p = 0.005)
  • Their excitement about AI enhancement was higher (+0.206 SDs, p = 0.029)
  • Their overall optimism about AI was also higher (+0.379 SDs, p = 0.000)
  • AI use simultaneously increases both fear and enthusiasm

Cross-national variation (跨國差異) in AI fears (Dong et al., 2026):

  • Fear of AI is occupation-specific (職業特定的) and varies across countries
  • Notable examples: unusually high fear of Religious AI in Saudi Arabia; unusually low fear of Careworker AI in Japan
  • Fear is driven by the mismatch (不匹配) between psychological traits (心理特質) people deem necessary for an occupation and the perceived potential of AI to possess those traits
  • Survey covered 20 countries; occupations assessed included: judge, doctor, journalist, careworker, religious leader

Exam trap: AI anxiety and positive attitudes toward AI are not mutually exclusive — the Noy & Zhang (2023) data show that the same ChatGPT users simultaneously reported higher worry about replacement AND higher excitement and optimism. Ambivalence is the expected finding, not contradiction.
Exam Traps Summary
  • Trustworthiness, trust, and trusting behaviour are distinct concepts — trustworthiness is objective; trust is a psychological state; trusting behaviour is the actual act of reliance.
  • Warmth and competence are independent dimensions of AI perception — high competence does not imply high warmth.
  • Cognitive offloading is not inherently negative; automation bias is the harmful form where verification and independent judgement are abandoned.
  • AI anxiety and positive AI attitudes are not mutually exclusive — people can simultaneously feel more worried about replacement and more optimistic about AI (ambivalence).
Top things to memorize
  1. Trust in AI predicts acceptance and intention to use AI (Choung et al., 2023); successful AI integration depends on worker trust (Glickson & Woolley, 2021)
  2. Three-stage trust process: actual trustworthiness → impression formation / trust → trusting behaviour
  3. Three components of AI trustworthiness (Bedue & Fritzsche, 2021): ability, integrity, benevolence
  4. Integrative trust framework (Li et al., 2024): trustor, trustee, and interactive context each contribute factors
  5. Humans treat AI as social actors: warmth (friendliness, sincerity, empathy) and competence (intelligence, power, efficiency) are the two most influential trust factors (Li et al., 2025)
  6. Post-ChatGPT: warmth attributions and human-likeness metaphors increased; search-engine and computer metaphors decreased (Cheng et al., 2026)
  7. Performance paradox: AI boosts immediate output but may reduce critical thinking, skill development, and unaided performance
  8. Cognitive offloading (r = +0.72 with AI use); cognitive offloading negatively correlated with critical thinking (r = -0.75) (Gerlich, 2025)
  9. Automation bias: over-reliance on AI output, cessation of verification — critical risk in high-stakes domains
  10. Skill atrophy evidence: programming study (Shen & Tamkin, 2026) — AI use decreased skills; cognitively engaging usage patterns (Generation-Then-Comprehension) yielded highest quiz scores (86%)
  11. Eight dimensions of AI anxiety (Li & Huang, 2020): privacy violation, bias behaviour, job replacement, learning difficulty, existential risk, against ethics, artificial consciousness, lack of transparency
  12. Cross-national fear of AI is occupation-specific and driven by mismatch between perceived human-only traits and AI's potential (Dong et al., 2026)
