Lecture 8: Questioning the Need for Principles

This lecture examines whether moral thought actually requires general principles, setting up the debate between Generalism and Particularism. The central challenge is Reasons Holism: the idea that the moral weight of a feature can shift depending on context, which puts pressure on any fixed rule-based system. Understanding how Generalists and Particularists each respond to this challenge is the key throughline of the lecture.

1. Generalism vs. Particularism

The core contrast in this lecture is between two positions on the role of principles (原則) in morality (道德):

  • Generalism (普遍主義) — morality depends on general principles; major theories (Utilitarianism, Kantian Ethics, Natural Law, Prima Facie Duties) all provide such principles
  • Particularism (特殊主義) — morality depends on the morally relevant features (道德相關特徵) of particular cases; these features may not behave in a fixed, principle-like way
  • Particularism does NOT mean moral judgment is mere personal feeling
  • Particularism does NOT mean all moral generalizations (道德概括) must be false

Two distinct particularist commitments:

  • Principle Eliminativism (PE) (原則消除主義) — there are no correct or true moral principles that represent moral criteria and that are needed to explain the deontic status (義務性地位) of particular actions
  • Principle Abstinence (PA) (原則節制主義) — a morally committed agent who wants to reach correct verdicts about particular cases should not rely solely on moral principles for guidance
  • PE and PA are logically independent — one can hold PE without PA, or PA without PE
Exam trap: Particularism is often confused with moral relativism or subjectivism. Particularism does not say moral judgments are merely personal feelings or that all moral generalizations are false — it says features may not have fixed polarity across contexts.
Exam trap: PE and PA are not the same thing and do not entail each other. A student who conflates them will misidentify what a particularist is committed to in any given argument.

2. Types of Moral Principles and the Generalist Picture

Two kinds of moral principles (道德原則):

  • Absolute Principle (AP) (絕對原則) — states that a certain kind of action is always right or always wrong regardless of circumstances; once a feature is present, the verdict is fixed; decisive (決定性的)
  • Contributory Principle (CP) (貢獻性原則) — states only that a feature counts for or against an action; the contribution can be outweighed by other considerations; defeasible (可推翻的)
  • The particularist can attack both types

Why is Generalism (普遍主義) attractive? Generalists believe principles serve two roles:

  • Explanatory role (解釋角色) — we want to know not just whether an action is wrong but why it is wrong
  • Practical role (實踐角色) — principles assume an authority of guidance for agents

Three key theses within the Generalist picture:

  • Generic Generalist Thesis (一般普遍主義論題) — there is a small set of fundamental morally relevant features that explain the rightness and wrongness of actions; moral principles express these explanatory features
  • Universal Relevance Thesis (普遍相關性論題) — if a feature is morally relevant in one case, it is morally relevant wherever it appears
  • Polarity Thesis (極性論題) — if a feature counts in favour in one case, it always counts in favour

Reasons Atomism (理由原子論) — the moral contribution of a feature is fixed independently of its context; if a feature has a positive or negative moral charge, it carries that charge across all contexts; atomists allow reasons to outweigh each other, but the polarity of each reason stays fixed

Thesis Core Claim Implication
Generic Generalist Thesis Small set of fundamental features explain rightness/wrongness Principles can capture moral reality
Universal Relevance Thesis Morally relevant features stay relevant across all cases Features do not become irrelevant by context
Polarity Thesis A pro-feature always counts in favour; a con-feature always counts against No polarity reversal is possible
Reasons Atomism Moral charge of a feature is context-independent Features carry the same weight in every situation

3. Reasons Holism: Reversal and Silencing

Reasons Holism (理由整體論) — the moral contribution of a feature may depend on context; a feature that is a reason in one case may be no reason in another; a feature that counts in favour in one case may count against in another. This variability of reasons (理由的可變性) is the central particularist claim against atomism.

Reversal (極性逆轉) — a feature has reversed when its moral polarity (道德極性) changes across contexts. McNaughton's public hanging example:

  • The fact that spectators would enjoy a public execution could normally count as a reason in favour of an action (pleasure is generally good)
  • In this context, the pleasure is sadistic (殘忍的快感) — the same feature can function as a reason against the action instead
  • Whether pleasure is a reason for or against an action cannot be settled in isolation from other features of the action

Reversal is distinct from outweighing (超越):

  • Rossian view (羅斯式立場) — the pleasure still counts positively, but is outweighed by the cruelty (殘酷性)
  • Particularist view — the pleasure does not count positively at all in this context; the moral contribution of the feature itself changes, not just its weight
  • The point is not that rules have exceptions; the point is that the moral contribution of a feature itself changes

Silencing (靜默) — a feature that usually matters becomes morally irrelevant in a particular context. Example:

  • Normally, promising to do something gives a reason to do it
  • A promise to help someone cheat on an exam: the fact of promising does not give a reason to cheat; the promise feature is silenced by the immoral content of the promise

Summary of how a feature can behave (lying as illustration, with four cases):

  • Morally relevant in favour of the action — e.g. lying to protect a friend from unjust violence
  • Morally relevant against the action — e.g. a student lying on a job application
  • Morally irrelevant in context — e.g. lying as part of a harmless game
  • Relevant in a way that depends on other features — e.g. a doctor withholding a harsh truth temporarily to prevent immediate panic
Exam trap: Reversal and outweighing are fundamentally different. The Rossian says the feature keeps its positive polarity but loses in a balancing contest. The particularist says the polarity itself flips. Conflating these two positions is a common exam error.
Exam trap: Silencing is not the same as outweighing either. When a feature is silenced it has no moral contribution at all — it is simply irrelevant. Outweighing still grants the feature positive or negative force, just insufficient force.

4. Generalist Responses to Reasons Holism

If reasons holism is correct, do we lose moral theory, moral knowledge, and moral guidance? Generalists argue no — they propose two strategies:

  • Atomistic Specification (原子式規範化) — when a principle faces a counterexample (反例), add the conditions needed to avoid the counterexample
  • Holistic Accommodation (整體性調適) — accept reasons holism but insist that moral principles are still possible and useful

Atomistic Specification illustrated with the promise example:

  • Simple principle: "If one has promised, one has a reason to keep the promise"
  • Specified version: "If one has promised, and the promise was not coerced, and the promised act is not immoral, and the promise has not been cancelled, and keeping it would not violate anyone's rights, then one has a moral reason to keep the promise"

The particularist challenge to Atomistic Specification — the specified principle mixes different kinds of things:

  • Reasons (理由) — considerations that count for or against an action; the real reason in the promise case is simply "I promised"
  • Enablers (促成條件) — conditions that allow a consideration to function as a reason; e.g. "the promise was not coerced"
  • Disablers (阻礙條件) — conditions that prevent a consideration from functioning as a reason; e.g. "the promised act is not immoral"
  • It is odd to say: "I should go because my promise was not extracted under duress" — duress absence is not the reason, it is an enabler
  • Adding enablers and disablers does not save the principle; it merely disguises holism within a pseudo-atomistic structure
Generalist Strategy Mechanism Particularist Objection
Atomistic Specification Add conditions to block counterexamples Extra conditions are enablers/disablers, not additional reasons — the principle is not genuinely atomistic
Holistic Accommodation Build holism into the principle structure The resulting principles may be so permissive that particularism and holistic generalism are hard to distinguish in practice
Exam trap: Students often think that adding more conditions to a principle just makes it more accurate. The particularist argues that the extra conditions are categorically different from reasons — they are enablers or disablers — so the specification strategy does not vindicate atomistic generalism.

5. Holistic Generalism and Its Limits

Holistic Generalism (HG) (整體性普遍主義) — accepts reasons holism while insisting principles are still possible. A holist principle has the form:

  • Feature F counts in a certain way when the surrounding context allows it to do so, and when no contextual factor silences, reverses, disables, or outweighs it
  • Example: "If an action is an instance of killing a rational agent (理性主體), and no other feature explains why this fact fails to be a reason against the action, and no opposing reasons collectively outweigh this reason, then the action is wrong in virtue of being an instance of killing a rational agent"

HG principles have three components, known as Double Defeasibility (雙重可推翻性):

  • A morally significant feature — e.g. the action is an instance of killing a rational agent
  • Holism clause (整體論條款) — takes potential silencing or reversal into consideration
  • Overriding clause (優先條款) — allows for ordinary moral conflict where other reasons collectively outweigh

Why is HG not just particularism?

  • In HG, principles still play an explanatory role (解釋角色) — explanation works by subsumption (歸攝), subsuming particular cases under a general principle
  • For the particularist, explanation works by narration (敘述性解釋) — showing how the morally relevant features of this concrete situation add up here
  • HG shows that Reasons Holism (RH) does not entail Principle Eliminativism (PE); the particularist needs a stronger argument
  • Specifically: moral explanation may not need principles even when principles are made holistic; and PE does not mean no moral generalization is ever true

6. The Particularist Claim: Discernment and Case Comparison

Even if moral generalizations are sometimes true, they are not the foundation of moral explanation. The practical consequence is Principle Abstinence (PA) (原則節制):

  • Principles can distort attention (扭曲注意力) and cause premature closure (過早結論) — an agent who mechanically applies a rule may miss morally relevant features of the particular case
  • Moral deliberation (道德審議) requires sustained attention to the particulars
  • Principles can be useful for guidance, especially in professional ethics (專業倫理): medical ethics uses informed consent (知情同意), non-maleficence (不傷害), confidentiality (保密), and respect for patient autonomy (尊重病人自主); public servants use fairness, accountability, transparency, and integrity
  • But principles should not replace discernment (識別力)

Moral Discernment (道德識別力) — a trained capacity to notice morally relevant features and understand their relations:

  • Developed through experience (經驗), comparison (比較), reflection (反思), and moral education (道德教育)
  • Particularism does not reject moral learning — the model is learning patterns of relevance from cases

Practical Grammar (實踐語法) analogy:

  • In learning a language, we do not always learn a single definition applied mechanically — we learn how the word is used across contexts, its grammar, tone, and flexibility
  • Moral concepts may work similarly: we recognize obvious cruelty (明顯的殘酷), subtle cruelty (細微的殘酷), justified harshness that is not cruelty, apparent kindness that is actually cruel

Case Comparison (案例比較) — the particularist method for moral deliberation:

  • Consider whether a certain disclosure of confidential information is justified by comparing it with other cases: a malicious leak (惡意洩露), a whistleblowing case (吹哨人案例), a medical emergency (醫療緊急情況), a betrayal of a friend (背叛朋友), a legal duty to report harm (法律上的舉報義務)
  • These comparisons inform judgment but do not mechanically determine later decisions
Exam trap: Particularism is sometimes read as rejecting moral learning entirely. It does not — it rejects the use of principles as the primary vehicle for moral guidance, but endorses learning through case comparison and the cultivation of discernment.
Exam Traps Summary
  • Particularism is not moral relativism or subjectivism — it holds that features can have genuine moral force but that force is context-sensitive.
  • PE (Principle Eliminativism) and PA (Principle Abstinence) are logically independent; do not assume one entails the other.
  • Reversal and outweighing are different: reversal means the polarity of the feature flips; outweighing means the feature retains its polarity but loses in a balancing contest.
  • Silencing is not outweighing: a silenced feature has no moral contribution at all; an outweighed feature still contributes, just insufficiently.
  • The Atomistic Specification strategy does not save generalism because the added conditions are enablers/disablers, not additional reasons — the principle is not genuinely atomistic.
  • Holistic Generalism (HG) is not the same as particularism: HG retains subsumptive explanation under principles; particularism uses narrative explanation from concrete features.
  • Reasons Holism (RH) does not entail Principle Eliminativism (PE) — HG shows principles can accommodate holism; particularism needs a further argument.
  • Particularism does not reject moral learning — it endorses learning from cases and cultivating discernment, not learning rules to apply mechanically.
Top things to memorize
  1. Generalism vs. Particularism: Generalism holds morality depends on principles; Particularism holds it depends on context-sensitive features of particular cases.
  2. PE vs. PA: Principle Eliminativism says no correct principles exist; Principle Abstinence says agents should not rely solely on principles — logically independent.
  3. Absolute Principle (decisive, fixed verdict) vs. Contributory Principle (defeasible, can be outweighed).
  4. Four Generalist theses: Generic Generalist Thesis, Universal Relevance Thesis, Polarity Thesis, Reasons Atomism.
  5. Reasons Holism: the moral contribution of a feature depends on context — the foundation of the particularist attack on generalism.
  6. Reversal: a feature's polarity flips across contexts (McNaughton's public hanging example — sadistic pleasure becomes a reason against, not for).
  7. Silencing: a feature that usually matters becomes morally irrelevant (promising to help cheat — the promise feature is silenced).
  8. Atomistic Specification vs. Holistic Accommodation: two generalist strategies responding to reasons holism.
  9. Reasons/Enablers/Disablers distinction: the particularist uses this to show that Atomistic Specification just hides holism inside a pseudo-atomistic structure.
  10. Double Defeasibility in Holistic Generalism: morally significant feature + holism clause + overriding clause.
  11. HG explanation by subsumption vs. particularist explanation by narration.
  12. Moral Discernment: trained capacity to notice morally relevant features, developed through experience, comparison, and reflection — the particularist alternative to mechanical rule application.

Lecture 7: Virtue Ethics

Virtue Ethics (德行倫理學) shifts the central question of morality from "what should I do?" to "what kind of person should I be?" It grounds moral evaluation in character traits rather than rules or outcomes, and culminates in Aristotle's account of the good life as eudaimonia (幸福). The lecture also covers Care Ethics (關懷倫理學) as a related character-centred approach that foregrounds relationships, dependency, and emotional attunement as the foundation of moral life.

1. What is Virtue?

  • Virtue (美德) — a relatively fixed trait of character (性格) or mind, not a momentary impulse
  • Involves dispositions (傾向) to think, reason, feel, and act in certain ways across a range of different circumstances
  • Central to the positive evaluation of the moral worth (道德價值) of persons — not merely what they do, but who they are
  • Virtues are acquired (後天習得的) by practice and exercise — they are not naturally endowed excellences (天賦才能)
  • Virtues can be hierarchically arranged (按層次排列): some are foundational (cardinal), others subordinate

Three components of a virtue (using honesty 誠實 as the example):

  • Intellectual component (知性成分) — correct beliefs about when telling the truth is appropriate
  • Affective component (情感成分) — disposed to feel and express approval toward those who tell the truth
  • Motivational-behavioral component (動機行為成分) — disposed to tell the truth and avoid telling lies

Cardinal virtues (主要美德):

  • Not dependent on other virtues; other virtues are subordinate (從屬的) to them
  • Greek tradition (希臘傳統): wisdom (智慧), courage (勇氣), temperance (節制), and justice (正義)
  • Christian tradition (基督教傳統): faith (信), hope (望), love (愛), prudence (審慎), fortitude (剛毅), temperance (節制), and justice (正義)

Exam trap: Virtues are acquired through practice, not innate. Never say virtues are naturally given excellences — the source explicitly contrasts them as "not naturally endowed."

2. Aristotle on Virtue and the Good Life

The Highest Good (最高善) — Nicomachean Ethics (尼各馬可倫理學):

  • All human activities aim at some end (目的) or good — this is the teleological (目的論的) premise
  • The highest good is the end most choice-worthy (最值得選擇的): it must be unconditionally complete (無條件完整的) and self-sufficient (自足的)
  • That highest good is eudaimonia (幸福/繁盛)

The Function Argument (功能論證):

  • The highest good of humans concerns their purpose (目的) or function (功能) as human beings
  • The function of humans concerns what is distinctive (獨特的) of such beings — rationality (理性)
  • Rationality is distinctive of humans; its exercise is central to understanding the human function
  • Therefore the highest good = a life of rational activity (理性活動) of the soul guided by virtue (美德)
  • The good of any thing is that thing performing its function well; for humans this means rational activity guided by virtue

Eudaimonia (幸福/繁盛):

  • Standardly translated as "happiness" or "flourishing," occasionally as "well-being" (幸福感)
  • "Flourishing" applies to animals and plants; eudaimonia is possible only for rational beings (理性存有者)
  • "Happiness" in ordinary use is subjectively determined (主觀決定的); eudaimonia is not — it is objective
  • A moralized (道德化的) or value-laden (充滿價值判斷的) concept of happiness
  • Living in accordance with virtue (依美德而活) is necessary for eudaimonia
  • For Aristotle — virtue is necessary but not sufficient (必要但不充分) for a eudaimon life
  • For the Stoics (斯多葛學派) — virtue is both necessary and sufficient (充分且必要) for a eudaimon life

Exam trap: Aristotle says virtue is necessary but NOT sufficient for eudaimonia (external goods also matter). The Stoics are the ones who say virtue is both necessary AND sufficient. Confusing the two positions is a classic exam error.

Doctrine of the Mean (中道原則):

  • Virtue concerns feelings (情感) and actions (行動) in which excess (過度) is error and deficiency (不足) is blamed; the intermediate condition (中間狀態) is correct and praised
  • "Virtue, then, is a mean insofar as it aims at what is intermediate" (NE, 1106b)
  • The mean is not arithmetically fixed — it is relative to persons and contexts, determined by practical intelligence

Phronesis (實踐智慧) — Practical Intelligence:

  • The capacity to determine the right manner of feeling and action in contexts that call for a virtuous response
  • A higher-order virtue (高階美德) — governs the exercise of all other virtues
  • Comes only with experience of life (人生經驗); distinguishes the morally mature adult from nice children

Unity of Virtues (美德的統一性):

  • Virtues are interdependent — truly possessing one virtue requires possessing the others
  • Example: a truly just (正義的) person must also be temperate (有節制的); without temperance she would be disposed to take more than her fair share, undermining justice
  • The example used: a military campaign where food and water must be rationed

Exam trap: Unity of virtues does not mean virtues are identical. It means that genuinely having one virtue entails having the others — you cannot be truly just while lacking temperance.

3. Virtue Ethics as a Moral Theory

Core claim of Virtue Ethics (VE):

  • Facts about virtuous agents (有德行的行為者) — their character traits and how traits influence reactions — are more basic than facts about right conduct (正確行為)
  • These facts explain why an action is right or wrong; character is foundational (基礎性的), not derivative

Qualified agent-based version (有資格的行為者版本):

  • Based on a hypothetically ideally virtuous person (理想有德行者) who possesses all morally significant virtues and consistently acts on them
  • Deontic rules derived from this ideal agent:
    • An action A in C is obligatory (義務性的) iff any ideally virtuous agent would not fail to do A in C
    • An action A in C is wrong (錯誤的) iff no ideally virtuous agent would perform A in C
    • An action A in C is optional (可選的) iff some ideally virtuous agent might or might not perform A in C

Virtue rules and conflict:

  • Each virtue yields a virtue rule (美德規則) — e.g., the virtue of kindness yields the rule "Be kind"
  • VE features a plurality (多元性) of cardinal virtues, hence a plurality of virtue rules
  • Conflicts arise when two or more virtue considerations are relevant and indicate incompatible (不相容的) courses of action
  • VE does not eliminate moral conflict (道德衝突) simply by having no "fundamental rules" — plurality creates its own conflicts

4. Evaluation of Virtue Ethics

Counterexample — the reforming liar:

  • A mendacious (愛說謊的) person becomes convinced mendacity (謊言癖) is a vice and resolves to change
  • He cannot simply will himself to stop lying; he must engage in a regimen (一套訓練方法): writing down lies, reflecting on their significance, rewarding himself on lie-free days
  • Problem: none of these concrete reform activities are characteristic of what an ideally virtuous agent would do — yet they are clearly the right thing for this person to do
  • VE's criterion (判準) — act as an ideally virtuous agent would — fails to capture the moral correctness of self-improvement strategies

Revised criterion and obstinate-person counterexample:

  • Revised criterion: an action A in C is obligatory iff any ideally virtuous agent would advise one not to fail to do A in C
  • Counterexample: an obstinate (固執的) person does the opposite of whatever anyone advises
  • An ideally virtuous agent, knowing this, might strategically advise the person NOT to do A, believing this false advice will prompt him to do A
  • Result: what an ideally virtuous agent would advise is not what the person ought to do — the revised criterion also fails

Criterion version Statement Counterexample Failure
Original A is obligatory iff ideally virtuous agent would not fail to do A The reforming liar's regimen Regimen is right but not what ideal agent does
Revised A is obligatory iff ideally virtuous agent would advise not failing to do A The obstinate person Agent gives strategic false advice; advice ≠ what person ought to do
Exam trap: The counterexamples target the specific formulation of VE's action criterion — they do not reject the value of virtue or character generally. Make sure to distinguish the counterexample's target from a wholesale rejection of VE.

5. Care Ethics

Origins and core claim:

  • Inspired by psychologist Carol Gilligan's (卡羅爾·吉利根) 1982 book In a Different Voice
  • Personal relationships (個人關係) — between parents and children, siblings (兄弟姊妹), friends, and spouses — are paradigmatic (典型的) for all of morality
  • These relationships involve care (關懷) as their defining moral feature

Justice vs. Care — comparison across three dimensions:

Dimension Justice Perspective (正義觀) Care Perspective (關懷觀)
Conception of the self (自我概念) Individuality (個體性) Relatedness (關係性)
Nature of moral problems (道德問題本質) Inequality (不平等) is the central issue Disconnection (疏離) and abandonment (遺棄) are central
Moral reasoning (道德推理) Impartiality (公正性) Sensitivity (敏感性)

Two senses of care (關懷的兩種意義):

  • Caring about (關心): being invested (投入的) in a thing or person — an attitudinal state
  • Caring for (照料): actions of a person in the context of care ethics — practical engagement

Scope of care relationships:

  • Not all forms of relationship and care are equally morally valuable — some forms of care stifle (壓抑) the development of the person
  • Care ethics is not necessarily restricted to existing personal relationships
  • Advocates argue responsibilities of care are global (全球性的); one ought to extend care to others beyond one's immediate circle

Joan Tronto's Five Care Stages (關懷的五個階段): (from Moral Boundaries 1993; Caring Democracy 2013)

  • Attentiveness 專注性 (Caring About) — recognizing that a need exists; overcoming moral blindness (道德盲目)
  • Responsibility 責任性 (Taking Care Of) — shifting from "someone should do something" to "I/We must ensure this is done"
  • Competence 能力性 (Care-Giving) — ensuring that the care provided actually meets the need
  • Responsiveness 回應性 (Care-Receiving) — considering the receiver's perspective; being alert to potential abuses of power (權力濫用)
  • Solidarity 團結性 (Caring With) — recognizing that care is a collective responsibility (集體責任) of citizenship

Exam trap: Know the correct label pair for each stage (e.g., "Responsiveness" = Care-Receiving, not Care-Giving). The stage names and their bracketed activity forms are frequently confused. Tronto's framework comes from two books — Moral Boundaries (1993) and Caring Democracy (2013).

6. Care Ethics as Moral Theory

Care ethics is not a complete moral theory:

  • Stephanie Collins (2020, 98): CE does not cover duties arising from harming others, receiving benefits, making promises and contracts, or non-interference duties
  • These are important duties but fall outside the scope of care ethics

Two care ethics principles (關懷倫理學的兩項原則):

Principle Statement Source
Dependency principle (依賴原則) When an important interest (重要利益) is unfulfilled, you are capable of fulfilling it, and doing so is not too costly — you have a responsibility to fulfill it. "Important interest" includes material and non-material needs. Collins (2015), chapter 6
Principle of subsidiarity (輔助原則) Shift actual delivery of care to the most local and personal levels whenever possible. Care for others by enabling them to care for themselves. Engster (2007), p. 58

  • Application of both principles must be guided by emotional attunement (情感共鳴/調諧)

Exam trap: The dependency principle requires capability AND low cost — it is not an unlimited duty to help. The subsidiarity principle is about delegation to the most local level, not about reducing care overall. These are often misread as either too demanding or too permissive.
Exam Traps Summary
  • Virtues are acquired through practice, NOT naturally given — do not describe them as innate or naturally endowed
  • Aristotle: virtue is necessary but NOT sufficient for eudaimonia. The Stoics say virtue is both necessary AND sufficient. These two positions are frequently swapped in exams.
  • Unity of virtues means interdependence, not identity — genuinely having one virtue entails having others, but the virtues are still distinct traits
  • VE counterexamples target the specific action criterion (the "ideal agent" standard), not the value of character generally
  • The reforming liar counterexample shows the original criterion fails; the obstinate-person counterexample shows the revised criterion also fails
  • Tronto's five stages: Attentiveness (Caring About), Responsibility (Taking Care Of), Competence (Care-Giving), Responsiveness (Care-Receiving), Solidarity (Caring With) — know both the stage name and the activity form
  • Dependency principle requires capability AND low cost — not unlimited; subsidiarity is about shifting care to the most local level, not reducing it
  • Care ethics is explicitly NOT a complete moral theory — it does not cover non-interference duties, promise-based duties, or duties from harm
Top things to memorize
  1. Aristotle vs. Stoics on eudaimonia: Aristotle — virtue necessary but not sufficient; Stoics — virtue necessary AND sufficient
  2. Three components of a virtue: intellectual, affective, motivational-behavioral (use honesty as the example)
  3. Function Argument steps: distinctiveness → rationality → rational activity of soul guided by virtue = highest good
  4. Doctrine of the Mean: virtue aims at the intermediate between excess and deficiency; determined by phronesis
  5. VE deontic framework: obligatory = ideal agent would not fail to do; wrong = no ideal agent would do; optional = ideal agent might or might not do
  6. Two VE counterexamples: reforming liar (targets original criterion) and obstinate person (targets revised criterion)
  7. Unity of virtues illustrated by justice + temperance in the military rationing scenario
  8. Justice vs. Care: self = individuality vs. relatedness; problem = inequality vs. disconnection; reasoning = impartiality vs. sensitivity
  9. Tronto's five stages in order: Attentiveness, Responsibility, Competence, Responsiveness, Solidarity (with their paired activity forms)
  10. Dependency principle (Collins): unfulfilled important interest + capable + low cost → responsibility to fulfill
  11. Subsidiarity principle (Engster): shift care delivery to the most local and personal level possible
  12. Care ethics is not a complete moral theory (Collins 2020) — it does not replace justice-based duties

Lecture 6: Kant on the Idea of Duty

Kant grounds morality not in human nature or happiness but in pure practical reason — the capacity shared by all rational agents to deliberate and act freely. This lecture traces the architecture of his moral system: from the Categorical Imperative and its two main formulas (Formula of Humanity and Formula of Universal Law), through a structured hierarchy of duties, to the final claims about moral worth, the highest good, and the practical postulates of immortality and God.

1. Foundations of Kantian Duty

  • Moral law (道德法則) holds for all rational agents (理性行為者) — not just humans; if non-human rational beings exist, they are equally bound.
  • Grounded in practical reason (實踐理性) — one's capacity to deliberate and make free choices — not in facts about human nature.
  • Key quote: "the ground of obligation must not be sought in the nature of the human being . . . but solely in concepts of pure reason" (G, 4:389).
  • Duty (義務) involves an action or omission one must perform regardless of what ends one happens to adopt.
  • Therefore morality cannot be a system of Hypothetical Imperatives (假言命令) — commands that depend on one's contingent desires.
  • And morality is not a doctrine of personal happiness (個人幸福學說).

Categorical Imperative (CI) vs. Hypothetical Imperative (HI):

TypeStructureValidity
Categorical Imperative (定言命令) Unconditional command — binds regardless of one's ends Absolute necessity
Hypothetical Imperative (假言命令) "If you intend E and A is necessary for E, then do A (or give up E)" Only conditionally valid

Exam trap: Morality for Kant is NOT grounded in human nature or in the pursuit of happiness — it is grounded in pure practical reason alone. Conflating Kant's ethics with happiness-based theories is a common error.

2. Formula of Humanity

Two kinds of ends (目的):

  • Ends adopted based on desires (欲望) — acquire value by being objects of choice; merely conditional worth.
  • Ends in themselves (自身目的) — have unconditional value independent of any choice.
  • Kant: "Rational nature exists as an end in itself" (G, 4:428–29).
  • Autonomy (自律) is the ground of the dignity (尊嚴) of human and every rational nature.
  • Freedom of choice — not any particular purpose — is what makes categorical imperatives possible: "they refer to no other property of choice . . . than simply to its freedom" (MM, 6:222).
  • Logical chain: unconditional value → unconditional requirement to respect it → our autonomous nature gives us that value → basic requirement to respect autonomy of all agents.

Formula of Humanity (FH 人性公式):

  • "So act that you use humanity, in your own person as well as in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means." (G, 4:429)
  • Negative part (消極部分): prohibits treating persons merely as a means (僅作工具) to one's ends.
  • Positive part (積極部分): requires promoting certain ends — self-perfection and the happiness of others.

Means vs. Mere Means (工具 vs. 僅作工具):

  • Asking a friend to drive you to the airport and the friend willingly agrees — friend is a means but not merely a means; morally permissible.
  • A mugger forcing the victim to hand over wallet at gunpoint — victim is treated as a mere means; morally wrong.
  • Regarding vs. Treating distinction: a gangster who internally regards the coffee seller as a mere means still does not act wrongly in the transaction of buying coffee — what is wrong is his attitude, not the act itself in that transaction.
  • FH also requires harmony: "It is not enough that the action not conflict with humanity . . . it must also harmonize with it" (G, 4:430).
  • Positive duties under FH: self-perfection (自我完善) and promotion of others' happiness (他人幸福).

Exam trap: Having a bad attitude toward someone (regarding them as a mere means) is NOT the same as treating them as a mere means in an action. The gangster buying coffee does not act wrongly even though his attitude is vicious.

3. Kant's System of Duties

Architecture (架構) of duties flowing from the CI:

  • CI → two general duties → more specific duties.
  • CI requires protecting and promoting humanity in oneself and others.
  • "The capacity to set oneself an end — any end whatsoever — is what characterizes humanity." (MM, 6:392)
  • Exercising this capacity requires developing certain powers → duty of self-perfection (自我完善義務).
  • All humans naturally wish to be happy; I recognize my own happiness as a legitimate end.
  • Taking others seriously as ends in themselves → I ought to take their ends as my own → duty to pursue others' happiness.

Perfect vs. Imperfect Duties (完全義務 vs. 不完全義務):

TypeDefinitionExample
Perfect duty (完全義務) One must never (or always) perform φ to the fullest extent possible — no discretion Do not lie; do not commit suicide
Imperfect duty (不完全義務) One must sometimes and to some extent perform φ — latitude in how and when Develop one's talents; help others in need

  • Vices (惡習) — attitudes that hinder us from fulfilling our duties.
  • Virtues (德性) — attitudes that facilitate our duties.

Levels of Duty and Casuistry (決疑論):

  • Kant identifies a hierarchy of levels of duty (義務層次), moving from the most abstract to context-specific applications.
  • Going from general principles to specific cases requires casuistry — a procedure of applying moral principles to particular situations.
  • "A metaphysics of morals cannot dispense with principles of application . . . in order to show in it what can be inferred from universal moral principles" (MM, 4:217).
  • When duties conflict: "The stronger ground of obligation prevails." (MM, 6:224)
  • Lizbeth's lie case: Lizbeth hides Tom from murderous gang members; telling the truth or refusing to answer would cause Tom's murder; she lies. This illustrates duty conflict resolution by the stronger obligation.

Exam trap: Perfect duties allow NO exceptions and NO latitude; imperfect duties allow latitude in how and when one fulfills them, but one cannot simply never fulfill them. Do not confuse "imperfect" with "optional."

4. Formula of Universal Law and Theory of Right Conduct

Formula of Universal Law (普遍法則公式 FUL) and Formula of the Law of Nature (FLN):

  • FUL: "Act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law." (G, 4:421)
  • FLN: "So act as if the maxim of your action were to become by your will a universal law of nature." (G, 4:421)
  • FH is a fundamental criterion of right action; FUL/FLN is a decision procedure (決策程序) to guide moral deliberation.
  • Maxim (格律): "a subjective principle of volition" on which an agent acts whenever she acts intentionally (G, 4:402n).
  • General semantic form of a maxim: "I will ________, if/when ________ in order to ________."
  • Maxim of commission (作為格律): "I will keep my promise whenever ________."
  • Maxim of omission (不作為格律): "I will omit to keep my promise whenever ________."
  • Universalizability test (普遍化測試): a maxim is universalizable if it can be consistently willed as a law of nature.

Theory of Right Conduct — Humanity Formulation:

  • Obligatory: A in C is obligatory iff failing to perform A in C would fail to respect humanity by treating oneself or others merely as means to a non-moral end.
  • Wrong: A in C is wrong iff performing A in C would fail to respect humanity by treating oneself or others merely as means to a non-moral end.
  • Optional: A in C is optional iff neither performing nor omitting A in C fails to respect humanity.

Theory of Right Conduct — Formula of Universal Law:

  • Obligatory: A in C is obligatory iff one cannot universalize the maxim of omission associated with A in C.
  • Wrong: A in C is wrong iff one cannot universalize the maxim of commission associated with A in C.
  • Optional: A in C is optional iff one can universalize both the maxims of commission and omission associated with A.

The False-Promising Example (虛假承諾例子):

  • A person in financial need wants to borrow money knowing he cannot repay; he considers making a false promise to repay.
  • Maxim of commission: "I will make a false promise to borrow money whenever I am in need."
  • If universalized: the very institution of promising (承諾制度) would collapse — no one would believe promises — so the maxim self-contradicts when universalized.
  • Result: false promising is wrong under FUL.

The Selfishness Example (自私例子):

  • Maxim: "I will refrain from helping those in need whenever I can help, even though I am well off."
  • Seems universalizable without strict contradiction.
  • But it conflicts with a maxim of rationality: "I will to be helped whenever I am in need and others who are well off can help me."
  • One cannot rationally will both maxims simultaneously → fails FLN.

Possible Objections (可能的反對意見) to FUL:

  • Universalizability test ≠ rule consequentialism (規則後果主義) — FUL does not evaluate actions by aggregate outcomes.
  • FH and FLN/FUL are not obviously equivalent formulations.
  • False negative: "Whenever the stock market climbs another 200 points, I will withdraw all my savings." — presumably not universalizable → deemed impermissible by FUL, but intuitively not morally wrong.
  • False positive: "I will get money on a false promise whenever it is March 8 and I can get it from someone named Igor Cycz to buy a metal detector." — presumably universalizable (too specific to generalize) → deemed permissible by FUL, but intuitively morally wrong.

Exam trap: For FUL, whether an action is obligatory, wrong, or optional depends on whether you can universalize the maxim of omission or the maxim of commission respectively — not just "the maxim" in general. Distinguish commission from omission maxims carefully.
Exam trap: The universalizability test is NOT rule consequentialism and FH and FUL are NOT obviously equivalent. Saying Kant's ethics reduces to "whatever produces the best outcomes when universalized" is wrong.

5. Moral Worth, the Highest Good, and Practical Postulates

Moral Worth (道德價值) and Dignity (尊嚴):

  • Autonomy has intrinsic unconditional value — this is dignity.
  • Since all persons are autonomous, they have equal value — one "cannot deny all respect to even a vicious man as a human being . . . even though by his deeds he makes himself unworthy of it" (MM, 6:463).
  • A virtuous person (有德者) is one who is disposed to act from the sole motive of duty (義務動機) whenever duty calls — this is the good will (善意志).
  • Virtue also requires inner strength (內在力量) enabling one to resist temptation to transgress the requirements of duty, so one can act consistently from a good will.

The Highest Good (至善 summum bonum):

  • Two components: (1) possession of virtuous character; (2) happiness befitting one's virtuous character.
  • "An impartial rational spectator can take no delight in seeing the uninterrupted prosperity of a being graced with no feature of a pure and good will." (G, 4:393)
  • The highest good is not mere virtue alone, nor mere happiness alone, but virtue accompanied by proportionate happiness.

Practical Postulates (實踐的懸設): Immortality and God:

  • Immortality (靈魂不死) as a practical postulate: achieving the highest good requires endless moral progress; endless progress requires continuous existence of the same rational being — hence the immortality of the soul is presupposed (CPR, 5:122).
  • God (上帝) as a practical postulate: happiness proportionate to virtue requires a cause adequate to produce that effect — hence we must postulate the existence of God (CPR, 5:124).
  • However, Kant insists that human beings cannot "cognize" (認識) God or immortality — they are not possible objects of sense experience (感官經驗); they are practical, not theoretical, postulates.

Exam trap: The postulates of immortality and God are practical (實踐的), not theoretical (理論的) — Kant does not claim we can know or prove their existence through reason or experience. They are presuppositions required for the coherence of moral practice, not metaphysical knowledge claims.
Exam Traps Summary
  • Morality for Kant is grounded in pure practical reason alone, NOT in human nature or happiness.
  • Having a bad attitude toward someone (regarding them as a mere means) is NOT the same as treating them as a mere means in one's action — the gangster buying coffee does not act wrongly.
  • Perfect duties allow no exceptions or latitude; imperfect duties allow latitude in execution but cannot simply be ignored forever.
  • For FUL, obligatoriness vs. wrongness depends on universalizing the maxim of omission vs. the maxim of commission respectively — distinguish the two.
  • The universalizability test is NOT rule consequentialism; FH and FUL are not obviously equivalent.
  • The postulates of immortality and God are practical postulates, not theoretical knowledge claims — Kant denies we can cognize them through sense experience.
Top things to memorize
  1. Moral law is grounded in pure practical reason, not human nature — holds for all rational agents.
  2. Categorical Imperative (unconditional) vs. Hypothetical Imperative (conditional on ends).
  3. Formula of Humanity: "Act so you use humanity always as an end, never merely as a means." — has negative part (no mere means) and positive part (promote self-perfection and others' happiness).
  4. Means vs. mere means distinction: a person can be used as a means without being a mere means; treating vs. regarding distinction.
  5. Perfect duty: no exceptions, must/never φ fully; Imperfect duty: must φ sometimes to some extent.
  6. Formula of Universal Law: universalizability test — cannot be rule consequentialism; FH and FUL not obviously equivalent.
  7. Maxim of commission vs. maxim of omission — determines wrongness vs. obligatoriness under FUL.
  8. False-promising: universalizing collapses the institution of promising (self-contradiction).
  9. Selfishness maxim: fails FLN by conflicting with a simultaneous maxim of rationality, not by strict logical contradiction.
  10. FUL objections: false negatives (stock market maxim) and false positives (Igor Cycz maxim).
  11. Highest Good = virtuous character + proportionate happiness; requires postulating immortality and God.
  12. Practical postulates (immortality, God) are practical, not theoretical — not objects of cognition or sense experience.

Lecture 5: Ethical Egoism

Ethical Egoism (倫理利己主義) holds that the rightness or wrongness of any action depends entirely on how it affects the self-interest of the person acting. This lecture maps its formal definition, distinguishes it from its cousins Rational Egoism and Psychological Egoism, examines the argument that tries to derive EE from PE, and then evaluates EE on consistency, determinacy, and external support.

1. Ethical Egoism: Definition and Core Properties

Ethical Egoism (EE, 倫理利己主義) is the theory that what makes an action right or wrong is how that action affects the self-interest (自身利益) of the individual who acts — not of anyone else.

  • Consequentialist (後果主義) — rightness is determined by outcomes, not intentions or rules
  • Welfarist (福利主義) — the relevant outcome is agent welfare (代理人福祉), not pleasure alone or preference satisfaction
  • Agent-Relative (以代理人為中心) / Agent-Centered — the welfare that counts is specifically the actor's own; other people's welfare does not enter the calculation
  • Maximizing (最大化) — the agent is required to produce the highest possible agent utility (代理人效用), not merely some acceptable level
  • Prudentialist (謹慎主義) — obligation is grounded in what is good for the agent, not in impartial concern

Formal theory of right conduct (正確行為理論):

  • Obligatory (義務性的): action A by person P in circumstances C is obligatory iff P's performing A in C would produce a higher agent utility than any other action P could perform instead
  • Wrong (錯誤的): A is wrong iff performing A in C would produce a lower agent utility than some other action P could perform instead
  • Optional (可選擇的): A is optional iff performing A in C would produce at least as much agent utility as any other action P could perform instead, and there is at least one other action that would promote at least as much agent utility as A

Master EE definition: An action A performed by P in C is right (not wrong) iff P's performing A in C would produce at least as much agent utility (代理人效用) as would any other action P might perform instead.

Exam trap: The EE definition of "right" looks identical to the definition of "optional action." The key difference: optional requires that at least one alternative also achieves the same utility level, making both permissible; the EE definition simply states what counts as right without requiring the existence of a tie — it sets the permissibility threshold, not a tie-condition.

Forms of Ethical Egoism

  • Objectivist EE: rightness depends on what actually maximizes agent utility
  • Subjectivist EE (SEE, 主觀倫理利己主義): action A is right iff P believes it would produce at least as much agent utility as any alternative — belief, not fact, is the criterion
VersionCriterion for rightness
Objectivist EEActual agent utility maximized
Subjectivist EE (SEE)Agent's belief that utility is maximized

2. Rational Egoism and Psychological Egoism

Rational Egoism (RE, 理性利己主義) addresses rationality (理性), not morality: an action A by P in C is rationally permissible iff performing A in C would produce at least as much agent utility as any other action P might perform instead.

  • RE asks: what makes an action rational or irrational? EE asks: what makes an action right or wrong?
  • They are "cousins" — the same self-interest criterion is used in both, applied to different normative domains
  • Their distinction matters in response to the objection that EE is not a genuine moral theory: a common assumption is that it is always rational to do what is morally right, so a moral theory aims to capture one kind of rational action; EE competes with any theory that denies all morally right action is necessarily self-interested action

Psychological Egoism (PE, 心理利己主義) is a descriptive (描述性) claim about human motivation: any action a human agent deliberately (刻意地) performs always has as its ultimate motivation (終極動機) that agent's self-interest.

  • Egoistic vs Altruistic motives (利他動機): motivated by desire to benefit yourself vs to benefit others
  • Ultimate vs Non-Ultimate motives: PE says self-interest is always the ultimate aim; intermediate goals can be altruistic in appearance
  • Distinct from Psychological Hedonism (心理享樂主義): PE allows ultimate motives beyond pleasure (e.g., power, reputation); hedonism restricts ultimate motive to pleasure
  • PE does not claim everyone consciously calculates self-interest at all times
  • People can hold false beliefs about what would actually promote their self-interest
  • PE distinguishes short-term from long-term motivation
  • PE refers only to deliberate (deliberate, 刻意的) actions — reflexes and compulsions are excluded
  • It is the ultimate object or target of a motive that determines whether it is self-interested, not the surface appearance of the act

Psychological Altruism (心理利他主義, PA) is PE's opponent: human agents can and sometimes do act from the ultimate motive of benefitting others. PA, like PE, is subject to empirical, scientific investigation (empirical, 實證的).

Exam trap: PE is a descriptive claim about motivation, not a normative (規範性的) claim about what one ought to do. EE is normative. Confusing the two is a common error — they are related but occupy different domains.
TheoryDomainClaim
Ethical Egoism (EE)Normative ethicsSelf-interest determines rightness
Rational Egoism (RE)RationalitySelf-interest determines rational permissibility
Psychological Egoism (PE)Descriptive psychologySelf-interest is always the ultimate motive
Psychological Altruism (PA)Descriptive psychologyAltruistic ultimate motives are possible

3. The Argument from PE to EE

The argument attempts to derive the normative conclusion (規範性結論) EE from the descriptive premise (描述性前提) PE, using the "ought implies can" (應該蘊涵能夠) principle as a bridge.

  1. If there are any actions one is morally obligated to perform, then one can perform them ("ought" implies "can" — 應該蘊涵能夠)
  2. If an action is one that one psychologically can perform, then it is an action whose performance is ultimately motivated by self-interest (自利, from PE)
  3. Therefore, if there are any obligatory actions, they are ultimately motivated by self-interest (from 1 and 2)
  4. There are actions one is morally obligated to perform
  5. All obligatory actions are ultimately motivated by self-interest (from 3 and 4)
  6. If all obligatory actions are ultimately motivated by self-interest, then all obligatory actions serve to maximize one's self-interest
  7. Therefore: all obligatory actions serve to maximize one's self-interest (= EE)
Exam trap: Step 6 is not obviously valid — being "ultimately motivated by self-interest" does not automatically mean the action maximizes self-interest. The argument contains a gap here that is easy to miss in exam conditions.

4. Is Psychological Egoism True?

Pro-egoistic explanations (支持利己主義的解釋) for apparently altruistic helping behaviour:

  • Aversive-empathic arousal reduction (厭惡性共情喚起減少): helping others is actually a means of reducing the helper's own discomfort or distress at witnessing suffering — self-relief, not other-benefit, is the real motive
  • Self-punishment avoidance (避免自我懲罰): the agent helps to avoid guilt or self-blame — again a self-interested motive
  • Social esteem / self-rewards attraction (社會聲譽吸引): the agent helps to gain social approval or internal self-reward

The American social psychologist Charles Daniel Batson (巴特森) designed experiments to test whether the aversive-arousal-reduction hypothesis was correct:

  • Participants observed a "worker" receiving uncomfortable electric shocks
  • One group: if they did not help, they would not have to continue observing (easy escape condition — egoism predicts they would not help)
  • Another group: if they did not help, they would continue observing (difficult escape condition)
  • Egoism predicts fewer participants would help in the easy-escape condition (since they can avoid discomfort without helping)
  • Result: that prediction was not confirmed — participants helped even when they could easily escape the aversive observation

Arguments for PE: Philosophical Versions

The "What You Most Want" Argument (最想要論證):

  1. Whenever anyone acts, they necessarily do whatever they most want to do, given their options
  2. If anyone necessarily does what they most want to do, then they are necessarily acting out of the motive of self-interest
  3. Therefore: whenever anyone acts, they necessarily act out of the motive of self-interest (= PE)
  • Rebuttal — P1 relies on the liking sense (喜好意義) of "want": what one wants = what one feels some attraction toward doing. Under this sense, P1 is false — people often act against their strongest felt attraction
  • Rebuttal — P2 relies on the explanatory sense (解釋意義) of "want": whenever one chooses, the behaviour can be explained by beliefs + a motivating desire. Under this sense, P2 can be false — the desire doing the explaining need not be self-interested

The Satisfaction Argument (滿足論證):

  1. The result of having desired goals met is that the desire is satisfied (滿足)
  2. Satisfaction is an experience involving some degree of positive feeling — if not pleasure then at least relief
  3. Therefore: whenever one acts, one's actions are ultimately motivated by the pleasure or relief one gets from satisfying the desire (= PE)

The Pleasure Argument (快樂論證):

  1. All desires are ultimately based on the motive of bringing about as much pleasure for oneself as possible and must be so based
  2. If one's fundamental motive is necessarily a desire for one's own maximal pleasure, then PE is true
  3. Therefore: PE is true
Exam trap: The Satisfaction Argument and Pleasure Argument are often conflated. The Satisfaction Argument infers self-interested motivation from the psychological fact that desire-fulfillment feels good; the Pleasure Argument asserts a stronger metaphysical claim that all desire is fundamentally pleasure-seeking. They are distinct and have different vulnerabilities.

5. Evaluation of Ethical Egoism

Consistency (一致性): EE appears at odds with the Principle of Non-Interference (PNI, 不干涉原則): "It is morally wrong to knowingly interfere with someone else doing their duty."

  • If my duty is to maximize my own self-interest and someone else's duty-performance conflicts with mine, EE seems to permit interference
  • Resolution options: (a) reject PNI outright, or (b) qualify PNI so it reads: "It would be morally wrong to knowingly interfere with someone else's performance of duty iff such interference would not be in one's own best interest" — subordinating PNI to self-interest

Determinacy and Usability (確定性與可用性): A moral theory's principles, together with relevant facts, should entail definite verdicts about rightness/wrongness across a large range of cases.

  • Problem: we do not know all the relevant facts about the full effects of various actions on the agent's overall self-interest
  • This parallels the standard objection to Utilitarianism (功利主義) — we cannot calculate all consequences
  • EE's response: under uncertain conditions, operate the calculus on expected agent utility (預期代理人效用) — the same strategy some utilitarians use with expected utility
  • EE can also adopt the distinction between critical (批判性) vs intuitive (直覺性) contexts of moral reasoning — use intuitive rules of thumb ordinarily, reserve full calculation for exceptional cases

External Support (外部支持): A moral theory should be consistent with, and ideally supported by, well-established theories and assumptions from other fields.

  • Batson's experimental findings (支持利他主義的實驗結果) appear to refute PE — people help even when self-interested escape is available
  • However, this does NOT refute EE — EE is a normative (規範性的) position about what one ought to do, not a descriptive claim about what people actually do
  • PA (Psychological Altruism) is a descriptive position; even if PA is empirically true, it tells us nothing directly about what is morally right
  • The normative/descriptive distinction (規範性/描述性區別) insulates EE from empirical psychology: facts about motivation do not straightforwardly determine facts about obligation
Exam Traps Summary
  • The EE definition of "right" resembles the definition of "optional action" — they differ: optional requires a tie with at least one alternative; EE's definition sets the permissibility threshold without requiring a tie
  • PE is descriptive (about motivation); EE is normative (about obligation). Do not conflate or use one to directly refute the other
  • Step 6 in the PE-to-EE argument (being ultimately motivated by self-interest implies maximizing self-interest) is a logical gap — it is not obviously valid
  • The Satisfaction Argument and Pleasure Argument are distinct: one infers self-interest from the phenomenology of desire-fulfillment; the other asserts a metaphysical claim that all desire is pleasure-seeking
  • Batson's experiments refute PE (or the aversive-arousal hypothesis), but they do NOT refute EE — EE is normative, not a claim about actual human motivation
  • PE is distinct from Psychological Hedonism: PE allows ultimate motives beyond pleasure; hedonism restricts the ultimate motive to pleasure only
Top things to memorize
  1. EE master definition: action A is right iff it produces at least as much agent utility as any alternative P might perform instead
  2. Five properties of EE: Consequentialist, Welfarist, Agent-Relative, Maximizing, Prudentialist
  3. Three-way distinction: obligatory (highest utility), wrong (lower than some alternative), optional (tie at top with at least one other)
  4. Objectivist EE vs Subjectivist EE (SEE): actual utility vs agent's belief about utility
  5. PE definition: every deliberate human action is ultimately motivated by self-interest
  6. PE vs Psychological Hedonism: PE allows ultimate motives beyond pleasure; hedonism does not
  7. PE is descriptive; EE is normative; RE is about rationality — three different domains
  8. PE-to-EE argument structure: "ought implies can" + PE → obligatory actions maximize self-interest
  9. Batson's experiment: easy-escape vs difficult-escape; egoism's prediction was not confirmed
  10. Three pro-egoistic explanations for helping: aversive-empathic arousal reduction, self-punishment avoidance, social esteem/self-rewards
  11. EE's response to determinacy objection: use expected agent utility + critical vs intuitive moral reasoning contexts
  12. Batson's findings threaten PE, not EE — EE is normative and insulated from descriptive empirical data

Lecture 4: Theoretical Objections to Consequentialism

Consequentialism faces a set of systematic theoretical objections grounded in clashes with deeply held moral intuitions. This lecture surveys those clashes, organises them around justice, rights, and demandingness, and then examines how consequentialists have attempted to respond — through bold denial, remote effects, satisficing, rule-based variants, and alternative theories of value.

1. Types of Objections to Consequentialism

The standard method of critique (批判) is to construct cases where the theory's verdict is dramatically at odds with considered moral beliefs (道德信念).

  • Two types of clash:
    • Clash with intuitions about justice (正義) and rights (權利) — cases where the theory seems to permit obviously unjust acts
    • Clash with intuitions about what is properly demanded (合理要求) — cases where the theory seems to require too much of agents

Structural Features of Classical Utilitarianism

Classical utilitarianism (古典效益主義) has three structural features that generate these conflicts.

Feature (特徵) Meaning
Direct (直接性) Actions are evaluated based solely on the utility (效益) directly associated with them
Maximizing (最大化) Rightness or wrongness depends on whether the action produces the highest net value (淨價值) compared to alternatives
Impartialist (公正性) Consequences for all affected parties are given equal weight (等量齊觀) in determining the deontic status (義務狀態) of an action
Exam trap: "Direct" refers to how actions are evaluated, not how consequences are calculated. Do not confuse "direct" with "immediate" — remote consequences still count under classical utilitarianism.

2. Justice and Rights Objections

The objection from retributive justice (應報正義) shows that utilitarianism can require punishing innocent people.

  • Framing case: A series of grave unsolved crimes has unsettled a town; residents threaten to riot if no perpetrator is found. Police have no viable suspects.
    • Option: frame a socially isolated (社會孤立) individual with a prior criminal record (前科) — someone against whom a credible case could be fabricated
    • Utilitarianism appears to entail that the police chief is morally obligated to frame and punish the innocent person

The medical sacrifice (醫療犧牲) case shows utilitarianism can require killing one to save many.

  • Organ harvest case: A patient admitted for alcohol abuse — no family, homeless, socially isolated — has healthy organs. Three other patients each urgently need a different organ transplant (器官移植).
  • Utilitarianism appears to require the physician to sacrifice the one patient to save the three.
  • Lazari-Radek and Singer variant: A surgeon performing delicate brain surgery could instead harvest organs because no one could suspect foul play if the patient dies on the table. The consequentialist, they argue, must accept that killing the one to save four is the right act.

The distributive justice (分配正義) objection targets utilitarianism's indifference to the distribution (分配) of utility across persons.

  • Threshold for a decent life: +500 utility
  • Below 0 = below the poverty line (貧困線), a life of constant struggle
  • Utilitarianism prefers Scheme 2 (higher aggregate) even if it requires ignoring how that utility is spread across individuals
  • No weight is given to equality of distribution (平等分配) as such
Exam trap: Utilitarianism sums total utility across persons — it does not maximise utility for the worst-off person. Confusing utilitarian aggregation with Rawlsian maximin is a common error.

The promising case (承諾情境) illustrates conflict between consequentialist calculation and the binding force (約束力) of a promise (承諾).

  • Jones, terminally ill, entrusts a large sum to the agent with a promise to give it to his daughter when she turns 21
  • By age 21 the daughter wins the lottery and has no need for the money; the agent's own circumstances have deteriorated badly
  • Consequentialism seems to require breaking the promise and keeping the money — yet this clashes with the moral force of the original commitment

3. Demandingness and Partiality Objections

The demandingness objection (嚴苛性反駁) argues that the impartialist standpoint of utilitarianism leaves no legitimate space for personal projects (個人計畫) or preferences.

  • Gardening case: The agent's partialist (偏私的) concern gives a good reason to spend Sunday gardening; the utilitarian impartialist standpoint demands volunteering for greater overall benefit instead.
  • The agent is pulled in two directions: personal interest vs. impartial obligation (公正義務)
  • Utilitarianism seems to always require the option with the highest aggregate utility, leaving no room for personal projects

The special obligations (特殊義務) objection targets utilitarianism's impartiality (公正性) with respect to close relationships.

  • Education case: The agent can pay for his son's college education or fund a more academically promising neighbour's child whose parents cannot afford it
  • If the neighbour's child would produce better consequences, utilitarianism requires funding the neighbour — not one's own son
  • This seems to deny the moral weight of special parental obligations (父母特殊義務)
Exam trap: The demandingness and special obligations objections are distinct. Demandingness concerns how much sacrifice is required; special obligations concern whether partiality toward loved ones is ever permissible. Do not conflate them.

4. Consequentialist Responses

Consequentialists have three broad strategies (策略) for responding to the objections.

  • Rebut the objections directly
  • Defend an alternative theory of welfare (福利理論)
  • Reject one or more of the three structural features (直接性、最大化、公正性)

Bold denial (大膽否定) accepts the counterintuitive verdict and challenges the authority of moral intuitions (道德直覺).

  • Applied to the promising case: intuitions that some things "simply cannot be done" are not reliable guides — they are post-hoc rationalisations (事後合理化)
  • Joshua Greene (2008, 63): "We have strong feelings… But it is not obvious how to make sense of these feelings, and so we… make up a rationally appealing story."
  • Intuitions may be products of evolution or cultural conditioning (文化制約), not moral truth

The remote effects (遠期效應) response argues that stipulating utility maximisation is implausible once long-term and social consequences are considered.

  • Applied to the framing case: would framing an innocent person actually maximise utility once risks of exposure, erosion of public trust (公共信任), and systemic damage to the justice system are factored in?
  • Applied to the medical sacrifice case (Lazari-Radek/Singer): the surgeon's action could damage social order if it came to public attention; thus it would not be recommended even if technically "right"
  • Limitation: the remote effects response does not work when cases are carefully stipulated to exclude such consequences

5. Structural Modifications: Satisficing and Rule Consequentialism

Satisficing consequentialism (滿意效益主義) replaces the maximising (最大化) requirement with a threshold (閾值) requirement.

  • To satisfice (滿意化) = to perform an action whose consequences are "good enough" even if some alternative would have even better consequences
  • SC formula: Action A in circumstances C is morally right iff the utility of doing A in C meets or exceeds the specified threshold utility (指定閾值效益)
  • Advantage: permits gardening on Sunday if it clears the threshold — relieves excessive demandingness

Satisficing consequentialism faces its own unacceptable implications (不可接受的含義).

  • Charity theft case: An agent collecting charity donations could keep a portion for themselves — the overall utility still exceeds the threshold (+150 vs. +200). SC deems this acceptable even though it is theft (竊盜).
  • Rescue case: Ian the helicopter pilot can save 6 campers or 2 campers from flooding; saving only 2 still meets the threshold. SC entails Ian does nothing wrong by saving 2 instead of 6.
  • These cases show SC is too permissive (過於寬鬆): it allows clearly wrong acts whenever they clear the threshold

Rule consequentialism (規則效益主義) replaces evaluation of individual acts with evaluation of codes of rules (行為規則).

  • Alternative codes of rules are evaluated by the value of the consequences associated with each code
  • Rightness or wrongness of an action depends on whether it is required or prohibited by an ideal code (理想規則集)
  • RC formula: Action A is right (not wrong) iff A is not prohibited by an ideal code of rules
  • Ideal code = the set of rules whose acceptance utility (接受效益) would be at least as great as any alternative code
Objection RC Response
Medical sacrifice A rule permitting physicians to sacrifice innocent patients would have lower acceptance utility than a rule prohibiting it; prohibition wins
Demandingness Given human disposition to favour oneself and close others, a rule of strict impartiality has high internalization costs; a less demanding rule scores higher
Promise-keeping Rule "keep promises" (R1) vs. "break if preferred" (R2) — R1 produces higher acceptance utility

Rule consequentialism faces the collapse objection (崩潰論證): iterating to ever-more-complex rules eventually reproduces act consequentialism (行為效益主義).

  • R1: Keep promises always
  • R3: Keep promises unless breaking saves innocent lives (higher value than R1)
  • In principle, a yet-more-complex rule can always be formulated to produce better results
  • R4: Keep promises unless some alternative would produce greater value — this is extensionally equivalent (外延等價) to act consequentialism; it agrees with AC on the deontic status (義務狀態) of every action
Exam trap: "Extensionally equivalent" means the two theories agree on which actions are right/wrong in every possible case — not that they use the same reasoning. R4 and act consequentialism produce identical verdicts even though R4 is stated as a rule.

6. Alternative Theories of Value

Nozick's experience machine (體驗機器) challenges hedonistic utilitarianism (享樂效益主義) by showing that subjective experience may not be all that matters.

  • Nozick (1974, 42): a machine stimulates your brain to make you feel you are writing a great novel, making a friend, reading — all while floating in a tank with electrodes
  • Question: Should you plug in for life?
  • Most people say no — suggesting that actual achievement (實際成就), real relationships (真實關係), and contact with reality matter beyond mere felt experience (感受體驗)
  • This challenges the hedonist (享樂主義者) assumption that only subjective states have intrinsic value (內在價值)

Desire fulfilment consequentialism (慾望滿足效益主義) replaces happiness with the fulfilment of desires (慾望滿足) as the measure of welfare (福利).

  • DF formula: Action A in C is right iff doing A would result in at least as much general desire fulfilment as any available alternative
  • Problem 1: Not all desire fulfilments contribute to one's welfare — e.g. a desire satisfied without the agent's knowledge may not benefit them
  • Problem 2: Fulfilment of horrendous (可怕的) desires does not seem to have intrinsic value

Perfectionist consequentialism (完善主義效益主義) holds that certain states make a life good without necessarily contributing to welfare or happiness.

  • Certain excellences (卓越性) — artistic, intellectual, moral — have intrinsic value independent of whether they produce pleasure or fulfil desires
  • Non-welfarist (非福利主義) version: the good is defined by objective standards of human flourishing (人類繁榮)
  • This form is closer to natural law theory (自然法理論) associated with Aquinas (阿奎那)
Theory of Value (價值理論) What counts as good? Key problem
Hedonism (享樂主義) Pleasure / positive experience Experience machine shows experience alone is insufficient
Desire fulfilment (慾望滿足) Satisfaction of desires Not all desires contribute to welfare; horrendous desires excluded
Perfectionism (完善主義) Objective human excellences Closer to natural law; raises question of who defines the excellences
Exam trap: Perfectionist consequentialism is still consequentialist — it evaluates actions by their outcomes. Do not confuse it with deontological natural law theory; the link to Aquinas is one of similarity in value theory, not structure.

  • The remote effects response fails when thought experiments are stipulated to exclude long-term consequences; bold denial is needed for such cases.

Lecture 3: Consequentialism

Consequentialism (後果主義) is the family of ethical theories that evaluates actions solely by their outcomes: what matters morally is how much value an action produces, not the intentions behind it or any rule it follows. This lecture traces the consequentialist framework from its three core structural theses through Classical and Hedonistic Utilitarianism, examines Mill's famous "proof" and its logical flaws, and then works through the tension between actual, expected, and subjective rightness, ending with the distinction between blameworthiness and objective wrongness.

1. The Three Theses of Consequentialism

Consequentialism (後果主義) is defined by three structural claims that together set it apart from deontological and virtue-based theories:

  • Value is independent of the right (價值獨立於正確性) — what is good or bad (value) exists prior to and independently of what actions are right; rightness is derived from value, not the reverse.
  • Explanatory priority of value (價值的解釋優先性) — the correct account of what makes actions right must appeal to value facts; value explains rightness, not vice versa.
  • Personal value alone as relevant (個人價值的相關性) — only the value that accrues to individuals (persons, sentient beings) counts when assessing outcomes.

Two further structural questions any consequentialist theory must answer:

  • Which theory of value? — What things are intrinsically good or bad? (e.g., pleasure, preference-satisfaction, objective goods)
  • How is right action related to value? — Is the right action the one that maximises value, satisfices it, or something else?
Exam trap: Students often write that consequentialism says "the ends justify the means" as if this is a complete definition. The structural point is more precise: value (好的結果) has explanatory priority over rightness — it is not just a slogan but a metaphysical claim about which concept is more fundamental.

2. Classical Utilitarianism: Structure and the Theory of Right Conduct

Classical Act Utilitarianism (行動功利主義) is built from four interlocking components:

Component Claim Key term
Maximising conception (最大化觀念) Right action = action producing the greatest total amount of value Right = greatest utility
Welfarism (福利主義) Welfare (福祉) is the fundamental value; only welfare matters intrinsically Welfare = intrinsic good
Universalism (普遍主義) The welfare of all individuals affected by an action counts — no one is excluded All affected individuals
Impartialism / Agent-neutrality (公正主義 / 行為者中立) Each person's welfare counts equally regardless of their relationship to the agent; the agent's own welfare gets no special weight Agent-neutral

Utility (效用) is defined as the net value of the consequences of an action:

  • Positive side: sum of positive value effects on all positively affected individuals.
  • Negative side: sum of negative value effects on all negatively affected individuals.
  • Net utility = positive value minus negative value across everyone affected.

The Theory of Right Conduct (正確行為理論) gives precise deontic statuses:

  • Obligatory (義務性) — action A in circumstances C is obligatory iff doing A in C produces higher utility than any other available alternative.
  • Wrong (錯誤) — action A is wrong iff doing A produces less utility than some other available alternative.
  • Optional (可選擇性) — action A is optional iff it is tied for first place (equal utility) with at least one other action.

Generic Principle of Utility (效用通則): An action A in C is right iff doing A in C would produce as high a utility as would any alternative action the agent could perform instead. This is the single formula that unifies the three deontic statuses above.

Exam trap: "Optional" in utilitarian terminology does not mean "morally irrelevant" — it means tied for the highest utility. Confusing optional with permissible-but-suboptimal is a common error. Optional actions have equal utility to the best available action, not merely acceptable utility.

3. Hedonistic Utilitarianism: Bentham and Mill

Hedonistic Utilitarianism (快樂主義功利主義) identifies welfare with happiness and happiness with the balance of pleasure (快樂) and pain (痛苦). Both Bentham and Mill accept two foundational claims:

  • One's welfare (how one fares in life) is a matter of one's level of happiness (幸福).
  • Happiness is constituted by experiences of pleasure and the absence of experiences of pain.
  • Pleasure is intrinsically good (本質上善); pain is intrinsically bad (本質上惡).

Ethical Hedonism (倫理快樂主義, EH): Experiences of pleasure are intrinsically good, experiences of pain are intrinsically bad, and they are the only items of intrinsic value with which ethics is concerned.

Principle of Hedonistic Utilitarianism (PHU): An action A in C is right iff doing A in C would produce at least as high an overall balance of pleasure versus pain as would any other alternative action open to the agent.

Bentham's Felicific Calculus (快樂計算法), introduced in An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), lists seven features for calculating the utility of any action:

  1. Intensity (強度) — how strong the pleasure or pain is.
  2. Duration (持續時間) — how long it lasts.
  3. Certainty and uncertainty (確定性與不確定性) — how probable the pleasure or pain is to occur.
  4. Propinquity and remoteness (近近性與遙遠性) — how near or far in time the pleasure or pain is.
  5. Fecundity (豐富性) — the probability that the pleasure will be followed by further pleasures.
  6. Purity (純粹性) — the probability that the pleasure will not be followed by pain.
  7. Extent (範圍) — the number of persons affected by the pleasure or pain.

Mill's Qualitative Hedonism (質的快樂主義): Mill refined Bentham's purely quantitative approach by arguing that some kinds of pleasure are qualitatively superior to others — not just more intense or longer-lasting.

  • Mill's criterion for quality (質量標準): If those who have experienced both types of pleasure consistently prefer one type even knowing it brings more discontent, then that pleasure is qualitatively superior.
  • The preferred pleasure outweighs the other by quality so much that quantity becomes comparatively small.

Two objections to Qualitative Hedonism (質的快樂主義批評):

  • Obj. 1 — Dilemma: Either quality is about the intrinsic nature of mental pleasure states, or it is not. If it is about intrinsic nature, then the only distinguishing features are intensity and duration — collapsing back into pure quantitative hedonism (量的快樂主義). If it is not about intrinsic nature, then Mill has abandoned hedonism altogether.
  • Obj. 2 — Expert judges problem: Competent judges must have developed capacities to enjoy the pleasures being ranked. But there is no guarantee that "expert judges" (專家評判者) will reach universal or even near-universal agreement about qualitative rankings.
Exam trap: Mill is often described as simply "adding quality to Bentham." But the critical point is that the two objections show this addition may be internally inconsistent — either quality reduces to quantity (making Mill identical to Bentham) or it breaks the hedonist framework entirely. Exam questions may ask you to diagnose which horn of Obj. 1's dilemma applies.

4. Mill's "Proof" and Its Fallacies

Mill acknowledges that "questions of ultimate ends do not admit of proof" but claims we can present considerations that lead the intellect to assent. His three-stage proof (三階段論證) attempts to establish that happiness (幸福) is the only intrinsic good:

Stage Conclusion Premises
Stage 1 Each person's happiness is an intrinsic good to that person. People desire their happiness for its own sake; if desired for its own sake, then desirable as such; if desirable as such, then intrinsically good.
Stage 2 General happiness (the happiness of all) is an intrinsic good to the aggregate (總和) of persons. Each person's happiness is intrinsically good to that person; therefore general happiness is intrinsically good to the aggregate.
Stage 3 Happiness is the sole (唯一) intrinsic good. All other things desired for themselves are only desired as parts of the end of happiness.

Mill's proof has been diagnosed as containing two classical logical fallacies (謬誤):

  • Stage 1 — Fallacy of Equivocation (歧義謬誤): "Desirable" carries at least two meanings: "able to be desired" (可被渴望的) and "worthy of being desired" (值得被渴望的). Mill slides from the empirical claim that people desire happiness to the normative claim that happiness is worthy of being desired. Analogy: "Philosophy is an art; art is studied by art historians; therefore philosophy is studied by art historians" — valid in form but the middle term shifts meaning.
  • Stage 2 — Fallacy of Composition (合成謬誤): From the fact that each part has a property, it does not follow that the whole has that property. Analogy: each part of a machine weighs less than five pounds; it does not follow that the machine weighs less than five pounds.
  • Stage 3 — Unclear reduction: Mill's own writings suggest that things like virtue and music are desired in and for themselves as parts of happiness, not merely as means to happiness. It is unclear how items desired in themselves can be "parts of the end of happiness" rather than independent intrinsic goods.
Exam trap: The Fallacy of Equivocation (Stage 1) and Fallacy of Composition (Stage 2) are separate diagnoses — do not conflate them. Stage 1 is a semantic slip between descriptive "can be desired" and normative "ought to be desired." Stage 2 is a logical leap from individual to collective without justification. Stage 3 is a conceptual unclarity, not a named formal fallacy.

5. Actual vs. Expected vs. Subjective Rightness

A practical objection (實際反駁) to PHU is the Problem of Ignorance (無知問題): to apply PHU in any concrete situation one would need to know the utilities of all available alternatives, which is often infeasible. A possible response: the principle of utility is a moral criterion (道德標準), not a decision procedure (決策程序).

Expected Consequences Utilitarianism (預期後果功利主義, ECU): An action A in C is right iff doing A in C has as high an expected utility (預期效用) as any alternative action the agent could perform instead. Expected utility = probability-weighted sum of outcomes.

Derek Parfit's Mine Shafts thought experiment illustrates the tension between actual and expected utility:

  • 100 miners (礦工) trapped underground, floodwaters rising; three remotely operable floodgates.
  • All miners are in either shaft A or shaft B; Phil does not know which; each is equally likely (0.5 / 0.5).
Phil closes If miners in Shaft A If miners in Shaft B Expected utility
Gate 1 Save 100 Save 0 (0.5×100)+(0.5×0) = 50
Gate 2 Save 0 Save 100 (0.5×0)+(0.5×100) = 50
Gate 3 Save 90 Save 90 (0.5×90)+(0.5×90) = 90
  • Intuitively Phil ought to close Gate 3 — highest expected utility, avoids risk of saving no one.
  • But suppose the miners were actually in shaft A: closing Gate 3 saves 90, not 100. Phil can say "I ought to have closed Gate 1 and saved all 100."
  • Both versions (actual and expected) pull on our intuitions in different scenarios — neither captures everything.

Subjective Principle of Utility (主觀效用原則, SPU): An action A in C is (presumptively) subjectively right (主觀上正確的) if — not iff — the agent believes that A has as high an expected utility as any of the available alternatives she believes are open to her.

  • SPU states only a sufficient condition (充分條件), not a necessary and sufficient condition (必要且充分條件) — note the "if" not "iff."
  • Without beliefs about expected outcomes, SPU will not guide action.
  • In some cases, even the presence of beliefs about expected outcomes does not warrant an act being subjectively right.
  • Constraint case: if closing Gate 3 saves only 50 regardless of shaft, expected utility = 50 for all three gates — yet intuitively Gate 3 is still subjectively right.
Exam trap: SPU uses "if" not "iff." This is deliberate and important — it means SPU gives a presumptively sufficient condition for subjective rightness, not a biconditional definition. Exam questions may present the SPU formula and ask what follows when the condition is absent or insufficient.

6. Constraints: Blameworthiness and Two Levels of Moral Thought

A crucial distinction (重要區分) in evaluating agents:

  • Blameworthiness / Praiseworthiness (可責備性 / 可稱讚性) are not the same as objective wrongness / objective rightness (客觀錯誤 / 客觀正確).
  • Moral praise and blame are sensitive to how morally conscientious (道德謹慎的) a person is when choosing an action.
  • Phil closes Gate 3, believing it best given available information; it turns out to be objectively wrong (objective utility was higher with Gate 1). Phil is nevertheless not blameworthy — he acted on the best available subjective judgment.

This yields two types of cases recognised by the framework:

Case type Subjective status Objective status Example
Blameless wrongdoing (無責備的錯誤行為) Subjectively right (did one's best) Objectively wrong (worse outcome than possible) Phil closes Gate 3; miners were in shaft A
Praiseless rightdoing (無稱讚的正確行為) Subjectively wrong (knowingly ignored better option) Objectively right (happened to produce best outcome) Lucky guess that produces maximum utility

R. M. Hare's Two Levels of Moral Thought (道德思考的兩個層次) address the objection that full utilitarian calculation is not always feasible:

  • Intuitive level (直覺層次) — ordinary moral life operates according to moral rules (道德規則) learned early on; we do not recalculate utility for every small decision.
  • Critical level (批判層次) — when ordinary moral rules conflict or a genuinely novel situation arises, we must engage in deliberate utilitarian reasoning about what to do.
  • Not all situations allow for moral deliberation; the two-level structure justifies rule-following in everyday contexts while preserving full utilitarian deliberation for hard cases.
Exam trap: Students frequently write that if an action is objectively wrong it must be blameworthy. The framework explicitly rejects this: blameworthiness tracks subjective (conscientious) rightness, not objective rightness. Similarly, an action can be objectively right yet not praiseworthy if the agent performed it without good-faith deliberation.
Exam Traps Summary
  • Consequentialism's three theses are structural/metaphysical claims, not just the slogan "ends justify means"; value has explanatory priority over rightness.
  • "Optional" in utilitarian deontic theory means tied for highest utility — not merely acceptable or permissible-but-suboptimal.
  • Mill's qualitative hedonism faces an internal dilemma: if quality is about intrinsic mental states, it collapses to quantitative hedonism; if not, it abandons hedonism.
  • Mill's "Proof" Stage 1 commits the Fallacy of Equivocation ("desirable" = capable vs. worthy); Stage 2 commits the Fallacy of Composition (individual good does not sum to collective good by logic alone).
  • SPU uses "if" not "iff" — it gives a sufficient condition for subjective rightness, not a biconditional definition.
  • Blameworthiness tracks subjective (conscientious) rightness, not objective rightness — an agent can be objectively wrong but not blameworthy, or objectively right but not praiseworthy.
Top things to memorize
  1. Three theses of consequentialism: value independent of right; explanatory priority of value; personal value alone relevant.
  2. Four components of Classical Act Utilitarianism: maximising, welfarism, universalism, impartialism/agent-neutrality.
  3. Generic Principle of Utility: A in C is right iff doing A produces as high utility as any alternative.
  4. Ethical Hedonism (EH) and Principle of Hedonistic Utilitarianism (PHU) — know both full formulations.
  5. Bentham's seven felicific calculus features: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent.
  6. Mill's qualitative criterion: consistent preference by competent judges, even at the cost of more discontent, establishes qualitative superiority.
  7. Obj. 1 dilemma against qualitative hedonism (intrinsic nature → collapses; not intrinsic → forsakes hedonism).
  8. Mill's Proof Stage 1 Fallacy of Equivocation; Stage 2 Fallacy of Composition.
  9. Mine Shafts: Gate 3 has highest expected utility (90) but is objectively suboptimal if miners are in shaft A; illustrates gap between actual and expected rightness.
  10. SPU is "if" not "iff" — sufficient condition only for subjective rightness.
  11. Blameless wrongdoing vs. praiseless rightdoing — blameworthiness tied to subjective, not objective, rightness.
  12. Two levels of moral thought: intuitive (rule-following) vs. critical (full deliberation when rules conflict).

Lecture 2: Natural Law Theory

Natural Law Theory (自然法理論) holds that morality is grounded in human nature and the purposes (目的) essential to it. Aquinas's framework combines a perfectionist theory of value, the Principle of Double Effect, and moral absolutism to determine which actions are obligatory, permissible, or wrong. Understanding these three components is key to applying NLT to real cases such as self-defence, euthanasia, and abortion.

1. Two Senses of "Law" and the Three Components of NLT

Natural law can be understood in two senses:

  • Descriptive (描述性) — regularities (規律) observed in nature; statements of fact about how things are
  • Normative / Prescriptive (規範性) — norms or rules for how people are supposed to behave
  • In NLT, "natural" means moral laws are grounded in human nature (人性), not merely in custom or legislation

NLT rests on three interlocking components (組成部分):

  • A perfectionist theory of value (完美主義價值論) — goodness consists in fully developing human nature
  • The Principle of Double Effect (雙重效果原則, PDE) — governs actions with both good and bad effects
  • Moral absolutism (道德絕對主義) — certain types of action are always wrong, regardless of consequences
Exam trap: Do not conflate the descriptive and normative senses of "natural law." A scientific law describing planetary motion is descriptive; NLT's moral laws are normative — they prescribe what humans ought to do based on human nature, not what they actually do.

2. Aquinas's Perfectionism and Value Theory

Aquinas's perfectionism (完美主義) connects a thing's essence (本質) with its purpose (目的):

  • Value of a thing = degree to which it is in the state that allows it to perform its function (功能)
  • This reflects a teleological worldview (目的論世界觀) — all things in nature have purposes (ends)
  • The goodness of a human being consists in realising the purposes characteristic (特有) of human beings
  • Human beings are rational agents (理性主體) with free will (自由意志) — it is up to us to understand our essential nature using reason (理性)

Value Perfectionism (價值完美主義) has two claims:

  • VP: The goodness of a thing is a matter of that thing fully developing those capacities (能力) essential to it
  • MP: A perfectionistic account of value is central to understanding the nature of right and wrong actions

Perfectionist theory of intrinsic value (內在價值) defines three categories:

Category Condition
Intrinsically good (內在善) State of affairs S is part of what perfects human nature
Intrinsically bad (內在惡) S hinders or destroys states of affairs that perfect human nature
Intrinsically value-neutral (內在中性) S neither perfects human nature nor hinders/destroys such perfection
Exam trap: Aquinas's perfectionism is a theory of intrinsic value — do not confuse it with instrumental value (工具價值). Something is intrinsically good if its realisation is itself part of perfecting human nature, not merely because it produces other good outcomes.

3. Four Basic Human Goods and the Fundamental Principle

NLT identifies four basic human goods (基本人類善) that are to be preserved and promoted:

Good (善) Implied Obligation (義務)
Life (生命) Protect and promote our own lives and the lives of others
Procreation (生育) Have and raise children
Knowledge (知識) Pursue knowledge
Sociability (社交性) Form friendship, marriage, and civil society (公民社會)

The Fundamental Principle of Right Conduct (正當行為基本原則) in NLT:

  • These four basic goods are to be preserved and promoted
  • Their hindrance (阻礙) and destruction (破壞) are to be avoided
Exam trap: All four basic goods carry genuine obligations — do not treat them as merely aspirational. The procreation good, for instance, implies an obligation to have and raise children, not just a permission.

4. Principle of Double Effect (PDE)

The Problem of Moral Conflict (道德衝突問題) arises when basic goods clash:

  • Kill-or-be-killed: may one kill in self-defence (自衛)?
  • Hysterectomy (子宮切除術): may a pregnant patient undergo the operation that will save her but kill the unborn child?
  • Lying to protect an innocent person from a potential killer: truth-telling vs. protection of life

NLT rejects pure consequentialism (後果主義) as the solution:

  • Consequentialism: choose the action producing the best overall results — only outcomes matter
  • NLT: intentions (意圖) also count; it matters whether an agent directly intends an evil effect

PDE: whenever an action produces at least one good effect and one evil effect, one is permitted to perform it iff ALL three conditions (條件) are met:

Condition (條件) Requirement
Intrinsic permissibility (內在許可性) The action, apart from its effects, must not be intrinsically wrong
Non-intentionality (非意圖性) The bad effect must not be intended — neither as an end nor as a means (手段) to the good effect
Proportionality (比例原則) There is a proportionally grave reason (充分嚴重理由) for bringing about the evil effect

Applying PDE to the hysterectomy case:

  • The operation is not itself morally wrong — intrinsic permissibility satisfied
  • The death of the unborn child is not the purpose of the operation, nor a means of achieving the good effect — non-intentionality satisfied
  • Saving the mother's life is a proportionally grave reason — proportionality satisfied
  • Conclusion: the hysterectomy is permissible under PDE

Applying PDE to the craniotomy (顱骨切除術) case:

  • Scenario: a pregnant woman with a heart condition will die unless her unborn child is aborted via craniotomy (crushing the skull)
  • The death of the unborn is a means (手段) of saving the mother's life — the bad effect is intended
  • Non-intentionality is violated — PDE does NOT justify craniotomy
  • Conclusion: craniotomy counts as a direct violation (直接違反) of the basic good of life
Exam trap: An effect can be intended in two ways — as a chosen end OR as a means to an intended end. Both count as intended under PDE. In the craniotomy, the child's death is a means; this fails non-intentionality even though the ultimate end (saving the mother) is good.

5. Moral Absolutism and Theory of Right Conduct

Moral Absolutism (道德絕對主義) in NLT:

  • Certain very general types of action are always morally wrong, even when they would produce good results
  • The intentional taking of human life (故意奪取人命) is absolutely prohibited — no exceptions

NLT distinguishes direct (直接) and indirect (間接) violations of basic values:

  • Direct violation: (1) the action seriously hinders or destroys a basic value, AND (2) the action cannot be justified by PDE
  • Indirect violation: satisfies clause (1) but IS justified by PDE — permissible under NLT

Theory of Right Conduct (正當行為理論) — formal definitions:

  • Obligatory (義務性): Failing to perform A would result in a direct violation of one or more basic goods
  • Wrong (錯誤): Performing A would result in a direct violation of one or more basic goods
  • Optional (可選): Neither performing nor failing to perform A would result in a direct violation of any basic good

Applications to end-of-life and self-defence (自衛):

  • Active euthanasia (主動安樂死): morally wrong — intentional killing of a human being, direct violation
  • Life-shortening pain medication: permissible — death is foreseen but not intended (justified by PDE)
  • Self-defence: using force against an aggressor (侵略者) is permissible (perhaps obligatory); but intending the aggressor's death is morally wrong
Exam trap: Moral absolutism says certain action-types are always wrong — but NLT still allows harmful side-effects via the indirect-violation route. The line is whether PDE justifies the act. Active euthanasia is a direct violation; prescribing lethal pain relief is an indirect violation and thus permissible.

6. Evaluation of NLT

Initial appeal (初步吸引力) of NLT — evaluation criteria:

  • Fit with moral beliefs: grounded in human nature; principles are objective (客觀)
  • Consistency and determinacy (一致性與確定性): arguably satisfied
  • Usability: applying PDE requires understanding its three elements; not trivially easy but workable

Challenges to moral absolutism (道德絕對主義的挑戰):

  • Is there any truly exceptionless (無例外) moral rule?
  • Active voluntary euthanasia (主動自願安樂死) and assisted suicide (協助自殺) strike many as morally justified
  • Case of Tony Bland: persistent vegetative state patient — sparked debate about whether intentionally ending such a life is ever permissible

Evaluation — Is PDE innate (天生的)? Moral nativism (道德先天論) argument:

  • Divergent intuitions (直覺) about the trolley problem (電車難題) suggest PDE may be built into human moral cognition
  • Bystander case: pull a lever to divert a runaway trolley from five workers to one — most say permissible (the one's death is a foreseen side-effect, not a means)
  • Footbridge case: push a large man off a bridge to stop the trolley and save five — most say wrong (using the man's body as a means)
  • Empirical results: bystander = permissible; footbridge = wrong — a distinction that mirrors PDE's non-intentionality condition
  • Harman 1999: "It is unclear how such a principle might have been acquired from the examples available to the ordinary person. This suggests that the relevant principle is built into [a person's] morality ahead of time."

Evaluation — The Core: Grounding Project (基礎論證) and its problems:

  • Aquinas: "All the things to which man has a natural inclination (自然傾向) are naturally apprehended as good" (ST, 49)
  • Logical gap: cannot validly derive normative (ought) statements from non-moral facts — the is-ought gap (實然–應然鴻溝)
  • Circularity (循環論證): identifying which inclinations are "good" already presupposes assumptions about the human good
  • Not all inclinations are good: aggression, cruelty are natural inclinations yet not morally endorsed

Evaluation — Moral Conflict (道德衝突) resolution within NLT:

  • One approach: find a non-arbitrary rank ordering (排序) of the basic rules
  • Another: appeal to the details of the case and use good moral judgment (道德判斷)
  • Neither approach is fully worked out within NLT

Evaluation — Moral Perfectionism (道德完美主義) alternatives:

  • One can agree that value considerations come before rightness without accepting perfectionism as the account of value
  • Alternative: welfare/well-being (福祉) as the central value rather than species-typical perfection
  • Raises the question: Can a plausible moral theory be based solely on human perfection?
Exam trap: The is-ought gap critique is not the same as the circularity objection. The is-ought gap says you cannot derive "ought" from "is" at all; the circularity objection says NLT's identification of good inclinations already smuggles in a prior value judgment about human good.
Exam Traps Summary
  • Do not conflate the descriptive and normative senses of "natural law" — NLT uses the normative sense grounded in human nature.
  • Aquinas's perfectionism is a theory of intrinsic value — do not confuse it with instrumental value.
  • All four basic goods carry genuine obligations, not mere permissions — including procreation.
  • An effect is intended under PDE if it is the chosen end OR a means to the intended end — both disqualify the act under non-intentionality.
  • In the craniotomy case, PDE fails because the child's death is a means, not merely a foreseen side-effect.
  • Moral absolutism forbids direct violations; NLT still allows harmful side-effects via the indirect-violation route (justified by PDE).
  • Active euthanasia = direct violation (wrong); life-shortening pain relief = indirect violation (permissible).
  • The is-ought gap objection and the circularity objection are distinct criticisms of NLT's grounding project.
Top things to memorize
  1. Two senses of law: descriptive (regularities) vs. normative (prescriptions grounded in human nature).
  2. Three components of NLT: value perfectionism, PDE, moral absolutism.
  3. Four basic human goods: Life, Procreation, Knowledge, Sociability — all to be preserved and promoted.
  4. Three conditions of PDE: intrinsic permissibility, non-intentionality, proportionality — ALL must be satisfied.
  5. Non-intentionality: bad effect must not be intended as an end or as a means.
  6. Direct violation = hinders/destroys a basic good AND cannot be justified by PDE; indirect violation = justified by PDE.
  7. Theory of Right Conduct: obligatory / wrong / optional defined by direct violation of basic goods.
  8. Hysterectomy permissible (PDE satisfied); craniotomy impermissible (child's death is a means — PDE fails).
  9. Active euthanasia wrong; life-shortening pain relief permissible under PDE.
  10. Trolley problem: bystander case permissible; footbridge case wrong — mirrors PDE's non-intentionality condition; Harman argues this suggests PDE is innate.
  11. Is-ought gap: cannot derive normative from non-moral factual claims; circularity: identifying good inclinations presupposes prior value assumptions.
  12. Alternative to value perfectionism: welfare/well-being as the central value.

Lecture 1: Moral Relativism

Moral relativism (道德相對主義) holds that what is right or wrong for a person is determined by the basic moral norms of their own culture, not by any universal standard. This lecture introduces the theory's core claims, distinguishes it from the weaker context-sensitivity thesis, examines the anthropologist's argument in its two versions, and then works through the major problems that challenge unrestricted relativism — culminating in a restricted version that tries to preserve cultural variation while blocking the most damaging objections.

1. The Theory of Right Conduct

Moral relativism (MR) defines obligation, wrongness, and permissibility entirely by reference to a culture's (文化) basic moral norms (基本道德規範):

  • Obligatory (義務的): action A, performed by a member of culture C, is obligatory iff the basic moral norms of C require A.
  • Wrong (錯誤的): A is wrong iff the basic moral norms of C prohibit A.
  • Optional (可選的) / Permissible: A is optional iff the moral norms of C neither require nor prohibit A.

MR contrasts with moral objectivism (道德客觀主義, MO):

  • MR: what is right or wrong for members of C depends entirely on C's own basic moral norms.
  • MO: there are at least some moral norms whose correctness is independent of what any culture does or might accept — they express objectively true (客觀真實的) standards for all cultures regardless of whether those cultures recognize or heed them.
Position Moral standard set by Universally binding?
Moral Relativism (MR) Each culture's own basic norms No
Moral Objectivism (MO) Culture-independent norms Yes
Exam trap: MR does not merely say moral beliefs differ across cultures — it says the moral facts themselves differ. Confusing descriptive variation in belief with the normative claim is a common error.

2. Context Sensitivity vs. Moral Relativism

The context sensitivity thesis (情境敏感性論題, CS) states that what is right or wrong for someone to do often depends on one's circumstances (情況), including facts about one's culture.

  • CS is not equivalent to MR.
  • Key difference: CS is compatible with MO; MR is not compatible with MO.
  • CS says context matters as an input to a universal standard; MR says the culture's norms are the ultimate standard itself.

Illustrative example — the drowning child (溺水的孩子):

  • An expert swimmer (專業游泳者) walking on a deserted beach sees a child drowning — they ought to rescue the child.
  • A non-swimmer in the same situation — they are not obligated to jump in, because doing so risks their own life.
  • Both verdicts flow from one objective principle (客觀原則): one ought to help those in need when one can do so without serious risk to oneself.
  • The difference in verdict is due to a difference in non-moral facts (非道德事實) about each agent's circumstances, not a difference in the underlying moral standard.
Thesis Context affects verdict? Compatible with MO?
CS Yes Yes
MR Yes No
MO Yes (through CS) —
Exam trap: Accepting that context changes what you ought to do does not commit you to MR. CS is the weaker claim that is fully compatible with objective universal principles — do not conflate the two.

3. Moral Diversity and the Anthropologist's Argument

The moral diversity thesis (道德多樣性論題, MD) makes two claims:

  • The moral codes (道德準則) of some cultures include basic moral norms that conflict with basic moral norms of other cultures.
  • Such fundamental conflicts (根本性衝突) are widespread.

Important logical point: MR does not entail MD.

  • Even if all cultures happened to accept the same basic norms, MR could still be true — the standard would still be set by cultural convention (文化慣例), not by objective facts.
  • Analogy: it could be universal to drive on the right-hand side and still be a matter of convention (慣例).

Anthropologist's Argument — Version 1:

  1. P1: Different cultures have different moral codes (= MD).
  2. P2: If MD is true, then there are no objectively true moral norms applying to all cultures.
  3. P3: If there are no universally valid moral norms, then MR is true.
  4. Conclusion: MR is true.

Challenges to Version 1:

  • Against P2: the mere difference in belief over some issue does not imply there is no single correct belief about that issue. Example: historical dispute (歷史上的爭議) about the shape of the earth — disagreement did not mean there was no fact of the matter.
  • Against P1: it is possible for cultures to differ in moral beliefs without there being fundamental moral disagreements — a deeper analysis is required to distinguish nonfundamental from fundamental disagreements.

Nonfundamental moral disagreement (非根本性道德分歧) — capital punishment (死刑) example:

  • Both parties share the same basic norm: a society ought to employ only punishments with overall uniquely beneficial consequences.
  • They disagree only on the non-moral empirical fact (非道德的經驗事實) of whether capital punishment actually has such consequences.
  • This is nonfundamental: the disagreement can in principle be resolved (可解決的) by examining empirical evidence.

Fundamental moral disagreement (根本性道德分歧):

  • One party holds: punishments should have overall beneficial consequences.
  • The other holds: punishments should inflict harm equivalent to harm caused (retributivism 報應論).
  • The disagreement is at the level of the basic norm itself, not the facts.

MD revisited — implications for the Anthropologist's Argument:

  • If intercultural moral disagreements can almost always be explained by differences in non-moral beliefs, MD's second claim (that fundamental conflicts are widespread) is false.
  • MD requires evidence of widespread fundamental disagreements, which is far from firmly established (遠未確立).
  • Key distinction: fundamental vs. nonfundamental disagreements differ in their resolvability (可解決性) by rational means.

Anthropologist's Argument — Version 2 (stronger):

  1. P1: Different cultures have different moral codes (= MD).
  2. P2: Such conflicts cannot be resolved by rational means (無法以理性方式解決).
  3. P3: There are widespread and rationally irresolvable intercultural conflicts over basic moral norms.
  4. P4: The best explanation for such irresolvable conflicts is that there are no objectively true moral norms for all cultures.
  5. P5: If there are no such objective norms (= denial of MO), then MR is true.
  6. Conclusion: MR is true.

An objectivist response (客觀主義者的回應) — the "chicken pull" (拔雞) case:

  • Suppose someone does not regard certain cruel treatment of animals as morally wrong, and shares all the same non-moral beliefs about animals.
  • Their moral code simply lacks a norm prohibiting such treatment.
  • The proper response is not relativistic acceptance: insist the individual is mistaken and urge them to exercise sympathy (同情心).
  • This suggests that at least some moral disagreements have a correct answer independent of cultural convention.

4. Problems of Formulation

Consistency problem (一致性問題):

  • A person can belong to multiple cultures simultaneously — e.g., Susan is a member of the Roman Catholic Church (羅馬天主教會), which forbids abortion (墮胎), and also of the Democratic Party, whose code condones abortion in many cases.
  • If MR is true, having an abortion would be both right and wrong for Susan at the same time — a contradiction (矛盾).
  • MR must specify which culture's norms take precedence, but gives no principled way to do so.

Usability and determinacy problem (可用性與確定性問題):

  • MR requires a determinate (確定的) account of what counts as a "culture" — without this, one cannot specify what makes an action right or wrong.
  • Criteria for identifying a culture are unclear: language? Ethnic origins? Religious affiliations (宗教歸屬)? Political views?
  • Without such a criterion, MR is not usable as a decision procedure.

5. Problems of Evaluation and Internal Support

Problem of intercultural evaluation (跨文化評估問題):

  • If MR is true, a culture's basic moral norms set the ultimate standard for right and wrong for that culture.
  • It would then make no sense to question the correctness of those norms from any external standpoint.
  • But surely it does make sense to criticize another culture's practices — this intuition (直覺) challenges MR.

Problem of intracultural evaluation (文化內部評估問題):

  • Preachers and politicians often speak of moral decline within a culture.
  • We may also claim our moral code has improved over time — e.g., regarding racial equality (種族平等) and gender equality (性別平等).
  • For a moral relativist, such comparative claims (比較性主張) make no sense, because the old norms were the correct standard at the time.

Moral reformer problem (道德改革者問題):

  • If MR is true, whenever a member of a culture attempts to reform its moral code and publicly opposes its practices, they are in the wrong by their own culture's standard.
  • This makes moral progress (道德進步) conceptually impossible under MR.

Internal support and explanatory power (內部支持與解釋力):

  • It is conceivable that a culture's basic moral norms conflict with one's considered moral beliefs (深思熟慮的道德信念) — Nazi Germany's (納粹德國) antisemitism (反猶太主義) is the example given.
  • Failure of internal support is simultaneously a failure of explanatory power — MR cannot explain why the individual moral reformer's judgment seems correct.
Exam trap: Students often think MR implies tolerance, so moral reformers would be tolerated. In fact, under unrestricted MR the reformer who opposes their own culture's norms is wrong by definition — tolerance of internal dissent is not built in.

6. Restricted Moral Relativism and Tolerance

Restricted Moral Relativism (限制性道德相對主義, RMR) attempts to preserve cultural variation while avoiding the major objections:

  • What is right and wrong for members of culture C depends on C's basic moral norms, but only in cases where those norms are consistent with universal constraints (普遍限制) on an adequate morality.
  • Those universal constraints properly reflect: (a) the functions of morality (道德的功能), and (b) facts about human nature (人性的事實).

Culture-independent constraints (獨立於文化的限制) include:

  • Intrapersonal (個人內部的) and interpersonal (人際的) functions of morality.
  • The satisfaction of physical needs (生理需求的滿足).
  • The goods of intimacy (親密), sociability (社交), and social status (社會地位).
  • Knowledge of the physical and human world (物理世界與人類世界的知識).

Interpersonal function of morality yields specific norms under RMR:

  • A norm of reciprocity (互惠規範): returning good for good.
  • Encouragement of other-regarding behaviour (關心他人的行為).
  • Allowance for personal freedom (個人自由).
  • Norms must be seen as justifiable (可辯護的) to members of the culture.

Key features of RMR:

  • Not "anything goes" (並非什麼都行): cultures whose norms violate the universal constraints are simply wrong.
  • Pluralistic (多元主義的): moral codes that imply different moral verdicts can both be equally correct or true, provided they both satisfy the constraints.

Relativism and tolerance (相對主義與寬容):

  • MR seems to commit one to a Principle of Tolerance (寬容原則): it is morally wrong for any culture to interfere with the moral practices of another culture.
  • The argument: if one wants to promote tolerance, one ought to accept MR.
  • But unrestricted MR is actually incompatible with the Principle of Tolerance — because tolerance itself would be a culture-relative value, and some cultures' norms may require interference.
  • Only RMR is compatible with the Principle of Tolerance, since tolerance can be grounded in the universal constraints.
  • Important: accepting MO or other non-relativist views does not automatically lead to intolerance — tolerance can also be grounded in objective moral principles.
Version of Relativism Allows cultural variation Compatible with Principle of Tolerance Blocks "anything goes"
Unrestricted MR Yes No (self-defeating) No
Restricted MR (RMR) Yes (within constraints) Yes Yes
Moral Objectivism (MO) Limited Yes (can be grounded objectively) Yes
Exam trap: It is a common mistake to think unrestricted MR supports tolerance. In fact it is self-defeating: if moral standards are set by each culture's norms, a culture whose norms require interfering with others cannot be told it is wrong to do so. Only RMR can coherently endorse a universal Principle of Tolerance.
Exam Traps Summary
  • MR claims moral facts differ by culture, not merely that moral beliefs differ — confusing descriptive variation with the normative claim is a basic error.
  • CS (context sensitivity) is compatible with MO; accepting that context changes what you ought to do does not commit you to MR.
  • MR does not entail MD: even universal moral agreement across cultures could coexist with MR if all norms were still set by cultural convention.
  • The Anthropologist's Argument v1 fails because: (i) disagreement does not imply no correct answer (vs. P2), and (ii) surface disagreements may be nonfundamental (vs. P1).
  • Nonfundamental disagreements rest on differing non-moral beliefs under a shared norm; fundamental disagreements are at the level of the basic norm itself.
  • Under unrestricted MR, a moral reformer opposing their own culture's norms is wrong by definition — the theory cannot accommodate moral progress.
  • Unrestricted MR is self-defeating with respect to tolerance: it cannot coherently mandate a Principle of Tolerance across all cultures.
  • Only RMR (not unrestricted MR) is compatible with the Principle of Tolerance; and accepting MO does not entail intolerance.
Top things to memorize
  1. MR definition: right/wrong for members of C is determined solely by C's basic moral norms; MO says at least some norms are culture-independent.
  2. CS vs. MR: CS says context affects verdicts but is compatible with MO; MR says culture's norms are the ultimate standard — the two are not equivalent.
  3. MD does not follow from MR — cultural convergence on norms is possible while MR remains true (convention analogy).
  4. Anthropologist's Argument v1: P2 fails (disagreement does not imply no correct answer); P1 fails (surface disagreements may be nonfundamental).
  5. Nonfundamental disagreement = same basic norm, different non-moral factual beliefs (resolvable empirically); fundamental = conflicting basic norms (not resolvable by facts alone).
  6. Consistency problem: a person belongs to multiple cultures with conflicting norms — MR generates contradictions.
  7. Determinacy problem: "culture" is undefined, making MR unusable as a decision procedure.
  8. Intercultural and intracultural evaluation problems: MR makes cross-cultural criticism and claims of moral progress conceptually incoherent.
  9. Moral reformer problem: under unrestricted MR, opposing your own culture's norms makes you wrong by definition.
  10. Restricted MR (RMR): cultural variation is valid only where norms satisfy universal constraints reflecting morality's functions and human nature.
  11. RMR is pluralistic (multiple codes can be equally correct if they satisfy constraints) but not "anything goes."
  12. Unrestricted MR is self-defeating regarding tolerance; only RMR can coherently endorse the Principle of Tolerance.
